A function of one number

One number goes in, one number comes out.

$$f(x)=\tfrac14 x^3-\tfrac34 x+1$$

Pick an \(x\), do the arithmetic, read off \(f(x)\).

  • \(f(0)=1\), \(f(1)=0.5\), \(f(2)=1.5\)
  • It climbs, then falls, then climbs again

Speaker notes

Spend a minute here and the rest of the section is easier. Slide the point and read values out loud: at \(x=0\) the value is 1, at \(x=1\) it is 0.5, at \(x=2\) it is 1.5. The dashed lines are the two coordinates of the point — one number in on the horizontal axis, one number out on the vertical.

This one curve is the example for the whole of section I, so say what to notice about its shape: going left to right it climbs, flattens out around \(x=-1\), falls through the middle, flattens again around \(x=1\), then climbs away. Where it flattens is exactly where the next few slides will find a derivative of zero, and the room will have seen it first as a shape.

Why a cubic and not a parabola: a parabola bends one way only. This one bends down on the left and up on the right, which the second-derivative slide needs.

It is also built out of the pieces the rules slides differentiate one at a time — \(x^3\), quartered, minus \(\tfrac34x\), plus 1 — so its derivative gets assembled in front of the room by the time the sum rule arrives.

Rates of change and the difference quotient

How much \(f\) changes, per unit of \(x\).

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Rise over run, between \(x\) and \(x+h\).

  • An average rate over one interval
  • The slope of the line through two points on the curve
  • Depends on \(h\) — until \(h\) is small

Speaker notes

Speed is the example everyone has: distance travelled divided by time taken is an average speed, and the shorter the interval the closer it gets to what the speedometer says. The difference quotient is that, for any function.

Now the slider, and this is the load-bearing minute of the section. Leave \(x\) where it is and pull \(h\) down slowly. Narrate two things at once: the line swings until it just grazes the curve, and the number in the readout stops moving. At \(h=1.5\) it reads one thing, at \(h=0.5\) another, and from about \(h=0.1\) down the digits stop changing. Say: whatever number that is, it belongs to the point and not to the interval. That is the next slide.

Then move \(x\) and do the pull again somewhere the curve bends the other way, and once where the curve is flat, so the settled number comes out negative and then near zero.

"As \(h\) shrinks" is the whole of the limit in this lecture. No epsilon, no delta. The arithmetic of actually shrinking it is done three slides on, with \(x^2\), where it comes out in one line. If asked when it fails: a corner, like \(|x|\) at zero, gives a different number from each side, and has no tangent.

The derivative

The number the quotient settles on.

$$\frac{df}{dx}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

The value the quotient approaches as \(h\) shrinks.

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\frac{df}{dx}\,h$$

Also written \(f'(x)\).

  • One number at each \(x\): the slope there
  • The best straight line, close up

Speaker notes

Two lines, and the second is the one to remember. The first says what the number is: the quotient from the previous slide, with the interval taken away. The second says what it is for — to predict \(f\) a little way from where you are, take the value you have and add slope times step. That is a linear function of \(h\), which is why the next lecture can do anything at all: it replaces the function it cannot see with the line it can.

Read the limit line out in words rather than symbols. "As \(h\) goes to zero" is shorthand for what the room just watched happen on the slider; it is not \(h=0\), which would divide by nothing. That is the entire treatment of limits in this lecture, and it is enough.

The prime is on the slide because the room will meet it everywhere else, and for no other reason. Say it once: \(f'(x)\), \(\frac{df}{dx}\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}f\) all name this number, and this series uses the middle one, because in section II there will be two things to differentiate with respect to and a prime cannot say which.

The slider magnifies the picture around the point, keeping the tangent drawn, and the readout gives the gap between the two at the edge of the frame — in units of \(f\), and in pixels, so it is not a matter of eyesight. At ×1 the curve is visibly a curve. At ×10 it still leaves the line by a good fraction of the frame. By ×100 the gap is a few pixels, and by ×1000 it is one. Zoom far enough and the curve is the line: that is what "smooth" means, and it is the thesis of the hour.

Worth one more sentence, on the two numbers falling at different rates: halve the window and the gap in \(f\) drops by four, not two — it goes like the square of the step, which is the precise sense in which the tangent is the best line. On screen the same gap only halves, because the picture doubled in size at the same time.

The slope of a constant

Nothing changes, so the rate of change is nothing.

$$\frac{d}{dx}c=0$$
$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\frac{2-2}{h}=0$$
  • Rise zero, over any run
  • The same answer at every \(x\)

Speaker notes

Four slides now, from the simplest function upwards, and each one is the difference quotient worked out. This is the first and it takes half a minute.

Put the arithmetic on the board with the slide: \(f\) is 2 at \(x\) and 2 at \(x+h\), so the rise is zero, so the quotient is zero, whatever \(h\) was. No limit is needed — it was already zero before \(h\) shrank.

Introduce the figure carefully here, because the next three slides reuse it. Upper panel: the function. Lower panel: its slope, drawn as a function of \(x\) in its own right. The dashed line runs down through both, so the dot below sits at the height of the slope up above. For a constant the lower panel is flat at zero, and that is the whole content.

Say the sentence the room needs to carry forward: the derivative is not one number, it is a function — a slope for every \(x\). The lower panel is that function. Everything after this slide is a way of working out what it is.

The slope of a line

The same slope at every point.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(kx+m\bigr)=k$$
$$\frac{\bigl(k(x+h)+m\bigr)-\bigl(kx+m\bigr)}{h}=\frac{kh}{h}=k$$
  • \(\frac{d}{dx}x=1\), \(\frac{d}{dx}7x=7\)
  • \(m\) moves the line, not its slope

Speaker notes

The arithmetic, out loud: go up by \(h\) in \(x\) and the value goes up by \(kh\), because that is what the \(k\) does. Divide by \(h\) and the \(h\) cancels, leaving \(k\). The \(m\) appears in both values and subtracts away, which is why shifting a line up does not change its steepness.

Two sliders. Move \(x\) first, with \(k\) left alone: the triangle slides along the line and its rise never changes, and the dot in the lower panel travels along a flat line. Then move \(k\): the line tilts and the lower panel steps up and down with it. A line has one slope; it is the number in front of the \(x\).

The two cases on the slide are the ones needed later: \(k=1\) gives 1, which is the power rule for \(n=1\), and \(7x\) gives 7, which is the constant-multiple rule two slides on. Both are worth saying aloud as instances of what is on screen.

If someone asks about \(k=0\): that is the previous slide, and the lower panel drops onto its axis.

The slope of \(x^2\)

The first function whose slope depends on where you are.

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^2=2x$$
$$\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h$$
  • Now the quotient keeps an \(h\) — and \(h\) shrinks away
  • Slope 2 at \(x=1\), 6 at \(x=3\), flat at the origin

Speaker notes

The first real derivative, and the only limit worth doing by hand in this lecture. Expand \((x+h)^2\) as \(x^2+2xh+h^2\); the \(x^2\) cancels against the one being subtracted; divide the rest by \(h\) and get \(2x+h\). Now say the sentence: the quotient depends on the interval only through that last \(h\), so as the interval shrinks, the answer is \(2x\).

Check it numerically, because it is the payoff of the slider two slides back. At \(x=1\): \(h=1\) gives 3, \(h=0.1\) gives 2.1, \(h=0.01\) gives 2.01, and the answer is 2.

Then the figure. Slide \(x\) and watch the two panels together: to the left of zero the tangent tilts down and the dot below is under the axis; at zero the tangent is flat and the dot is on the axis; to the right it tilts up and the dot climbs. The lower panel is a straight line through the origin of slope 2 — that is \(2x\), and the room can see it is not a constant any more.

Worth naming: the slope at \(x=3\) is 6, which is steeper than the curve ever looks in this frame, because the frame is showing about two units either way.

Rule: powers

The pattern that \(x^2\) started.

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n=n\,x^{n-1}$$

Bring the exponent down in front, and take one off it.

$$\frac{d}{dx}x=1,\qquad\frac{d}{dx}x^2=2x,\qquad\frac{d}{dx}x^3=3x^2$$
  • A constant is \(x^0\): the rule gives zero

Speaker notes

One rule covering the last two slides and everything polynomial after them. The room has seen \(n=1\) and \(n=2\) done from the quotient; assert the pattern and show it on the buttons rather than proving it.

If anyone wants the argument: expand \((x+h)^n\). The first term is \(x^n\) and cancels, the next is \(n x^{n-1}h\), and every term after that carries at least \(h^2\), so divides down to something with an \(h\) still in it and shrinks away. What survives is \(n x^{n-1}\).

Work the buttons in order. At \(x^1\) the lower panel is flat at 1 — the previous slide. At \(x^2\) it is a line through the origin. At \(x^3\) it is a parabola that never goes below zero, which says \(x^3\) never falls: check that against the upper panel, which climbs everywhere and is level only at the origin.

The \(x^0\) remark is worth ten seconds: any constant is \(x^0\), the rule gives \(0\cdot x^{-1}\), which is zero, and that agrees with the constant slide. Do not dwell on the \(x^{-1}\).

Rule: constant multiples

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(c\,f\bigr)=c\,\frac{df}{dx}$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl(\tfrac14 x^3\Bigr)=\tfrac14\cdot 3x^2=\tfrac34 x^2$$
  • Stretching a curve vertically stretches its slopes
  • Quarter the height, quarter the slope

Speaker notes

The quickest of the rules. Multiplying every value by a quarter multiplies every rise by a quarter and leaves every run alone, so it multiplies every quotient by a quarter. Nothing survives the division.

The figure is the running example's first piece. Both tangents are drawn at \(x=1.5\): \(3x^2\) there is \(6.75\), and a quarter of that is \(1.6875\). The flatter curve has the flatter tangent, by exactly the factor the curve was squashed by.

This is half of what "differentiation is linear" means, in last week's sense of the word: it respects scaling. The next slide is the other half. The \(7x\) from the line slide was this rule with \(f=x\), if anyone wants the connection.

Rule: sums

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(f+g\bigr)=\frac{df}{dx}+\frac{dg}{dx}$$
$$\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl(\tfrac14x^3-\tfrac34x+1\Bigr)=\tfrac34x^2-\tfrac34$$

That is the derivative of the example function, assembled from the three rules.

Speaker notes

Add two functions and the rises add, so the quotients add, so the derivatives add. Say it in that order; it is three steps long.

The figure is the payoff of the last five slides. The example function is the sum of a cubic piece and a straight piece, drawn separately, and the dashed line at \(x=2\) reads all three off at once: the pieces stand at \(2\) and \(-0.5\), and \(f\) sits at \(1.5\), their sum. The slopes there add the same way: the cubic piece has slope \(3\), the straight piece has slope \(-0.75\) everywhere, and \(f\) has slope \(2.25\). Check it against the formula: \(\tfrac34(4)-\tfrac34=2.25\).

Now sanity-check the shape claim from the first slide of the section. The derivative \(\tfrac34x^2-\tfrac34\) is zero at \(x=\pm1\) — the two places where the room said the curve flattens — negative between them, where it falls, and positive outside, where it climbs. The picture and the formula agree.

Product and quotient rules exist and are in every textbook. Nothing in the next three lectures differentiates a product of two unknown functions, so they are not taught here. What the series does differentiate, constantly, is a function of a function — two slides on.

Rule: the exponential

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x$$

The function that is its own rate of change.

$$\text{at }x=0:\;\text{slope }1\qquad \text{at }x=1:\;\text{slope }e\approx2.72$$
  • Its slope at every point is its height there
  • Taken as a fact today; \(e\) is the number that makes it true

Speaker notes

The odd one out, and the only rule here that is not derived. It is on the slide because lecture 4 needs it: the sigmoid and the softmax are built out of \(e^x\), and differentiating them is differentiating this.

The figure is the whole content. The tangent at 0 has slope 1, where the curve has height 1; the tangent at 1 has slope \(e\), where the curve has height \(e\). Wherever the curve is twice as high it is twice as steep, which is what proportional growth means and why this function describes compound interest, populations and radioactive decay.

If someone asks where \(e\) comes from: among all the curves \(a^x\), each has a slope proportional to its height, and \(e\approx2.718\) is the base for which the constant of proportionality is exactly 1. That is the definition of \(e\), and there is nothing more to it than a choice of convenient base.

The chain rule

One function fed into another.

$$u=g(x),\quad y=f(u)\;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx}=\underbrace{\frac{df}{du}}_{\text{at }u=g(x)}\cdot\;\frac{dg}{dx}$$

The outer rate, at the value the inner one hands it, times the inner rate.

$$u=\tfrac12x^2,\;\;y=\tfrac12u^2\;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx}=u\cdot x=\tfrac12x^3$$
  • Check: \(y=\tfrac18x^4\), and the power rule gives \(\tfrac12x^3\)

Rates compose by multiplying.

Speaker notes

The second load-bearing slide, and the one lecture 4 stands on entirely. Set it up as a two-stage machine before writing anything: \(x\) goes in, the inner function turns it into \(u\), and the outer function turns that into \(y\). The question is how fast \(y\) moves when \(x\) does, and the answer is the two rates multiplied.

The example is built so that both rates come from the power rule and the answer can be checked without the rule at all. Inner: \(u=\tfrac12x^2\), so \(\frac{dg}{dx}=x\). Outer: \(y=\tfrac12u^2\), so \(\frac{df}{du}=u\). Multiply: \(u\cdot x\), and \(u\) is \(\tfrac12x^2\), so the answer is \(\tfrac12x^3\). Then the check that settles it for the room: substitute first, \(y=\tfrac12(\tfrac12 x^2)^2=\tfrac18x^4\), and the power rule gives \(\tfrac12x^3\). Same answer, two routes.

Both curves are drawn against \(x\) on the same axes: blue is the inner function, red is the whole thing. Each carries its tangent, so both slopes are on screen at once. At \(x=1\) the inner rate is 1 and the outer rate is \(\tfrac12\), so the red tangent is flatter than the blue one — a rate of less than one shrinks what comes through. At \(x=2\) both rates are 2, red's slope is 4 against blue's 2, and the composite has overtaken the function inside it. That is the whole slide: the factors multiply, and neither one alone tells you the answer.

The trap, worth naming: \(\frac{df}{du}\) is evaluated at \(u\), not at \(x\). At \(x=2\) it is \(u=2\), not 2 because \(x=2\); the two agree here by accident, so pick \(x=1.5\), where \(u=1.125\), and read the readout.

Many books write this as \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}\), where the \(du\) looks as if it cancels. Mention it, because they will see it, and say what it is: a memory aid that happens to work, not the reason the rule is true.

Forward reference, said plainly: a neural network is a chain of a dozen functions, and its derivative is a dozen factors multiplied. Lecture 4 is about the order to multiply them in.

The second derivative

The slope of the slope.

$$\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}=\frac{d}{dx}\frac{df}{dx}$$

The derivative is a function, so it has a derivative too.

$$\frac{df}{dx}=\tfrac34x^2-\tfrac34\;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d^2f}{dx^2}=\tfrac32x$$
  • Positive: the curve bends upward
  • Negative: it bends downward

The first derivative is the tilt. The second is the bend.

Speaker notes

Three panels now, each one the slope of the one above it. Take the marker for a slow walk from left to right and read all three at once.

Start at \(x=-1.6\). Top: the curve is climbing. Middle: the dot is above the axis, which says the same thing. Bottom: the dot is below the axis, and that is the new information — the climb is easing off. Keep going. At \(x=-1\) the middle dot touches zero and the top curve is level. Past it the middle dot goes negative and the curve falls, while the bottom dot is still negative: the fall is getting steeper. At the origin the bottom dot crosses zero, and from there the fall eases, flattens at \(x=1\), and turns into a climb that keeps getting steeper.

So: the middle panel says which way the curve is going, and the bottom panel says whether that is increasing or decreasing. The arithmetic is one line — differentiate \(\tfrac34x^2-\tfrac34\) by the power and constant rules and get \(\tfrac32x\) — and the bottom panel is that straight line.

Say the word curvature once, and do not labour the interpretation: the bigger the bottom number, the tighter the bend, and the sooner the tangent line stops being a good approximation. That is the gap the zoom slider measured.

Forward reference, and do not do it here: lecture 3 uses the sign of this number at a level point to tell a valley floor from a hilltop, and its several-variable version, the Hessian, to choose step lengths.

A function with vector input

Several numbers in, one number out.

$$f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$$

For the rest of this section: two numbers in, one out.

$$f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2$$
  • \(f(1.5,\,0.8)=2.25+1.28=3.53\)
  • A height above every point of the plane: a landscape

Speaker notes

Say the change of shape explicitly: section I had one number going in. From here two go in — a point of the plane — and one comes out, a height above that point. Everything in this section is a way of asking "which way is uphill, and how steeply".

This is the shape that matters most in the series, and worth a sentence of motivation: a loss, a cost, an error, a score is always a single number produced from many inputs, and the whole of lecture 3 is about walking downhill on one.

Work the example arithmetic on the figure. The dashed lines in the base plane pick out \((1.5,\,0.8)\) — that is the input, two numbers. Go straight up from there to the surface and the height is \(2.25+1.28=3.53\) — that is the output, one number. Every figure in this section stands at that point.

Rotate it. Push the view down towards the horizon and it is two parabolas crossed; lift it towards straight down and the rings flatten into the map of level curves that the gradient slide uses. Do that lift now, so the word "level curve" lands on something they have watched appear.

Why the bowl is stretched: \(y\) is charged double, so walking in \(y\) climbs faster and the rings are tighter that way. A round bowl would hide every interesting thing in this section, and lecture 3 uses exactly this stretch for its zig-zag slide.

Partial derivatives

Hold every input still but one, and differentiate as before.

Hold \(y=0.8\)

$$\begin{aligned} f(x,\,0.8)&=x^2+2(0.8)^2=x^2+1.28\\[6pt] \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}&=2x+0=2x\\[4pt] &=3\quad\text{at }x=1.5 \end{aligned}$$

Hold \(x=1.5\)

$$\begin{aligned} f(1.5,\,y)&=(1.5)^2+2y^2=2.25+2y^2\\[6pt] \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}&=0+4y=4y\\[4pt] &=3.2\quad\text{at }y=0.8 \end{aligned}$$

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative, with the other inputs frozen.

Speaker notes

No figure on purpose. Do the arithmetic slowly, in both columns, because this is the whole of the idea and it is easier than it looks.

Left column. Freeze \(y\) at \(0.8\). Then \(2y^2\) is \(2\times0.64\), which is \(1.28\) — a number, not a variable. So the function of the one remaining input is \(x^2+1.28\), which is a section I function: a square plus a constant. Its derivative is \(2x\) by the power rule, plus zero for the constant, by the slide on constants. At \(x=1.5\) that is 3.

Right column, the same move with the roles swapped. Freeze \(x\) at \(1.5\) and \(x^2\) becomes \(2.25\), a constant that differentiates away, leaving \(4y\) from \(2y^2\) by the power and constant-multiple rules. At \(y=0.8\) that is \(3.2\).

Then the notation, which is the only new thing here: \(\partial\) instead of \(d\), and it means nothing more than "there were other inputs, and they were held still". One partial derivative per input, so two here.

Say what the two numbers are not: they are not the slope of the surface. They are the slopes along the two axis directions, and 3 against \(3.2\) already says the \(y\) direction is the steeper of the two. The gradient slide puts them together into one object and answers what the slope of the surface means.

One slice at a time

Each slice is a curve from section I.

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,\,y)-f(x,\,y)}{h}$$

The definition from section I, with \(y\) left alone.

  • Cut the surface along one axis: an ordinary curve
  • At \((1.5,\,0.8)\): slope 3 along \(x\), \(3.2\) along \(y\)

Speaker notes

The previous slide did the arithmetic; this one says what it was doing. Cut the landscape with a vertical plane along the \(x\) direction. The cut is an ordinary curve — one number in, one number out — and it has an ordinary slope. That slope is \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\). Cut along \(y\) and get the other one.

The definition on the slide is the one from the derivative slide with a second input sitting in it untouched. Point at the \(y\) in both terms: it is the same \(y\) in each, so it contributes nothing to the difference. That is the frozen input, written down.

Left panel: the two cuts on the surface, each with its tangent, in red for the \(x\) direction and green for \(y\). Right panel: the same two curves drawn flat, against the distance walked from the point. The green one is visibly the steeper, which is the \(3.2\) against 3 from the previous slide.

If asked why the cuts are drawn through a point off-centre rather than through the bottom of the bowl: because at the bottom both slopes are zero and there would be nothing to see. Lecture 3 is about getting there.

The gradient

The partial derivatives, collected in one row.

$$\nabla f=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\end{pmatrix}$$

A row, so that \(\nabla f\,h\) is a matrix times a step.

$$x^2+2y^2:\quad\nabla f=\begin{pmatrix}2x & 4y\end{pmatrix}$$
  • It points the way that makes \(f\) grow fastest
  • At right angles to the level curve through the point

The gradient is an arrow in the input plane, not on the surface.

Speaker notes

The third load-bearing slide, and the object lecture 3 is built from. The two numbers from the worked slide, written side by side, are the gradient — and then the claim that they are the coordinates of something geometric.

Where it lives is the thing people get wrong, so the figure puts it in the base plane: the red arrow lies flat, under the surface, because it is a direction to walk in, and you walk in the input plane. Its length says how fast the height climbs when you do. Nothing about it points out of the plane.

Move the point with the sliders and say what does not change: the arrow always crosses the level curve at a right angle, and it always points to higher ground. The faint vertical at its tip is the proof of the second half — the readout gives the height there against the height at the point, and it is always the larger. Then tilt the view towards straight down, where the right angle is a right angle on screen rather than a projected one, and say that this flattened view is the contour map every optimization paper draws.

On the arrow's length: it grows with \(\lVert\nabla f\rVert\) until it would leave the frame, and then stops. The readout carries the true length, and it is worth watching as the point moves out towards the steep wall of the bowl.

The reason for the right angle, in one breath: along the level curve the height does not change, so that direction contributes nothing, so it has zero product with the gradient — and zero product means perpendicular, in last week's sense.

Read it at \((1.5,\,0.8)\): \(\nabla f=(3,\;3.2)\), length \(\sqrt{9+10.24}\approx4.39\). Note it does not point at the origin, where the bottom of the bowl is. It points at the steepest way up here, and that gap between "steepest" and "towards the bottom" is the zig-zag lecture 3 spends a slide on.

On the row: the gradient is written lying down because that is the shape that multiplies a step vector, and because on the Jacobian slide it turns out to be a Jacobian with one row. The arrow in the figure is that row stood up; outside of matrix products nobody minds. The arrow is drawn at a third of its true length so it fits the frame.

The Hessian

All the second derivatives, in a matrix.

$$H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}$$

Differentiate each partial derivative in each input again.

$$x^2+2y^2:\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}$$
  • Symmetric: the order of the two derivatives does not matter
  • The diagonal is the bend along each axis

The Hessian is the bend of the landscape, in every direction at once.

Speaker notes

The second derivative of section I, with a vector going in. There is one for each pair of inputs, and they fit in a square matrix.

Do the four entries out loud. \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x\): differentiate in \(x\) again and get 2; differentiate it in \(y\) and get zero, because \(2x\) does not mention \(y\). Likewise \(4y\) gives 4 in \(y\) and 0 in \(x\). Twos and fours on the diagonal, zeros off it, and the same at every point of the plane, because this bowl bends the same everywhere.

The figure cuts the bowl along both axes through its lowest point, which is where the two bends are easiest to compare. The red cut is \(z=x^2\) and the green one is \(z=2y^2\): same shape, one twice as tight as the other. That is the 2 and the 4, and the stiff direction is the tight one.

Those two numbers are the eigenvalues of \(H\), from last week's eigenvalues slide, and the two cut directions are its eigenvectors. For a bowl tilted off the axes they would not lie along the axes, but they would still be the stiff and slack directions. The off-diagonal zeros are what makes this bowl easy: the two coordinates do not interfere. A nonzero corner entry means a valley running diagonally, and that is the hard case.

On shapes, if anyone asks how a matrix can belong to a function with one output: the gradient of \(f\) is a function that takes a point of the plane and returns a row of two numbers — a vector in, a vector out, which is the next section. The Hessian is that function's Jacobian, which is why it is square. Park it until the Jacobian slide, where the shapes get stated properly.

Two forward references, both for lecture 3. Newton's method needs the Hessian, because it models \(f\) near a point as a bowl of its own — \(f(x)+\nabla f\,h+\tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}Hh\) — and jumps to that model's bottom. And the ratio of the largest bend to the smallest, here 2, is the condition number, which decides how badly steepest descent zig-zags.

A function with vector output

Several numbers in, several numbers out.

$$f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$$

\(m\) ordinary functions of the same inputs, stacked in a column.

$$f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x+\tfrac14y^2\\[2pt] y+\tfrac14x^2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad f(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}1.25\\1.25\end{pmatrix}$$
  • Not a landscape any more — a map that moves the plane about

Speaker notes

Third and last shape, stated as plainly as the other two: several numbers in, several numbers out. A function like this is just \(m\) ordinary functions of the same inputs, written in a column — the top entry is one scalar function of \(x\) and \(y\), the bottom is another. Nothing is new except the stacking.

Where they turn up, said now so the shape does not look academic: the residuals of a fit, one per data point, are a vector-valued function of the parameters — that is lecture 3's least squares. A layer of a network takes a vector of activations to a vector of activations — that is lecture 4, every layer. And the gradient itself, from the last slide, is one of these.

Arithmetic on one point: \(f(1,1)\) has top entry \(1+\tfrac14=1.25\) and bottom entry the same, by symmetry.

The figures are the honest picture: the function takes the whole plane and pushes it somewhere. Grid lines go in straight and come out bent, because the map is not linear — last week every map sent straight lines to straight lines, and this one does not.

The question the next slide answers: the map bends the grid, so what is its derivative? Not one number, and not one row. One matrix, and the trick is to look at a small enough piece.

The Jacobian

One partial derivative for every input–output pair.

$$J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j},\qquad f(x+h)\approx f(x)+J\,h$$

Row \(i\) is the gradient of output \(i\). Shape: \(m\times n\).

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}1 & \tfrac12 y\\[2pt] \tfrac12x & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}$$

Locally, every smooth map is a linear map: its Jacobian.

Speaker notes

Build the matrix in front of the room, entry by entry, because the subscripts are the only hard part. Top output is \(x+\tfrac14y^2\): in \(x\) it gives 1, in \(y\) it gives \(\tfrac12y\). That is the first row, and it is the gradient of the top output. Bottom output is \(y+\tfrac14x^2\): in \(x\) it gives \(\tfrac12x\), in \(y\) it gives 1. Second row. At \((1,1)\) the off-diagonal entries are both \(0.5\).

Then the picture. Last week a matrix was a map of the plane, and its columns were where the basis vectors went. Here the grid is bent, but look at the small square: it has come out a parallelogram, to the eye. A linear map did that, and the linear map is the Jacobian. The dashed green outline is \(J\) applied to the square; the red one is the true image. They differ by less than the line width.

Now the slide to settle every shape question in the lecture, and do it out loud. \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\) has an \(m\times n\) Jacobian: one row per output, one column per input. One output makes it a single row — that is the gradient, which is why the gradient was written lying down. One input and one output makes it \(1\times1\), a single number, which is \(\frac{df}{dx}\). And the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient map, which takes \(n\) numbers to \(n\) numbers, so it is \(n\times n\). Four objects, one definition.

Lecture 3 uses the Jacobian of a residual vector for Gauss–Newton; lecture 4 has one per layer.

The chain rule, in several variables

$$J_{f\circ g}(x)=J_f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\;J_g(x)$$

Outer Jacobian, at the inner value, times inner Jacobian.

  • The chain rule again, with matrices in place of numbers
  • The shapes must agree — and they do

Rates compose by matrix multiplication.

Speaker notes

Chain rule, second time. A small step \(h\) in \(x\) becomes \(J_gh\) in the middle space, by the previous slide. That step becomes \(J_f(J_gh)\) in the last space. Two linear maps applied in turn are one linear map, their product — the matrix-product slide from last week — so the Jacobian of the composition is the product of the Jacobians.

In one variable the matrices are \(1\times1\) and this is the chain rule of section I, with the middle space one-dimensional. Point at the shapes: the middle dimension is what cancels in the product, exactly as the middle function's value was the thing handed over there.

The shapes on the figure: \(J_g\) is \(3\times2\), \(J_f\) is \(4\times3\), and \(4\times3\) times \(3\times2\) is \(4\times2\), which is what a map from \(\mathbb R^2\) to \(\mathbb R^4\) must have. Order matters: \(J_gJ_f\) does not even have compatible shapes.

This slide is backpropagation, stated. A network is \(f_L\circ\dots\circ f_1\), its derivative is \(J_L\cdots J_1\), and lecture 4 is about the fact that a product of matrices can be evaluated left-to-right or right-to-left, and that the cost differs enormously. Section IV starts on why.

By hand, by difference quotient, by program

Three ways to get the number.

Symbolic

d/dx x⁴/8
  = x³/2
  = 4     at x = 2

Exact. Done once, by a person or a computer algebra system.

Numerical

(f(2 + h) − f(2)) / h
  = 4.31  at h = 0.1
  = 4.03  at h = 0.01

Any \(f\) you can evaluate. Approximate, and the choice of \(h\) matters.

Automatic

x = (2, 1)
u = x·x/2
y = u·u/2
  = (2, 4)

Exact, to rounding. The program computes the derivative alongside the value.

Speaker notes

Everything so far says what a derivative is. A program needs the number. Three ways.

All three columns compute the same thing: the derivative of the chain-rule example, \(y=\tfrac12u^2\) with \(u=\tfrac12x^2\), at \(x=2\). The answer is 4, and the three columns differ only in how they get there.

Symbolic is what section I did: apply the rules, get a formula, type it in. Exact, but somebody has to do it for every function, and for a function of a million parameters written as code nobody will.

Numerical is the difference quotient with a finite \(h\), and it works on any black box you can call. The next three slides are the three ways to place the two sample points.

Automatic differentiation is the one most people have not met. The program that computes \(f\) is run as written, but every number carries its derivative along with it, and every operation updates both by the rules of section I. No formula is ever produced and nothing is approximated.

The forward difference

Step forward from \(x\).

$$\frac{df}{dx}\approx\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

The difference quotient, stopped at a finite \(h\).

  • Two evaluations: \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+h)\)
  • Error roughly proportional to \(h\): halve \(h\), halve the error
  • Cheapest, and the usual first choice

Speaker notes

Same example function as section I, and the same picture as the difference-quotient slide — but the question has changed. There the interval was shrinking towards an idea; here it stops at whatever \(h\) the program can afford, and what is left over is an error to be measured. The dashed green line is the true tangent, so the gap between the two lines is the error.

At \(x=1.4\) with \(h=1\), the chord slope reads \(2.02\) against a true \(0.72\). That is not a subtle error — the chord is reaching all the way out to \(2.4\), where the curve is far steeper. Halve \(h\) to \(0.5\) and the error roughly halves; halve it again and again the same. Say the rule as the room watches it: error proportional to \(h\).

Where it comes from, in one sentence: the curve leaves its tangent at a rate set by the bend, so the error is about \(\tfrac{h}{2}\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}\) — bigger step, bigger miss, and worst where the curve bends hardest.

Note that the estimate is one-sided: it knows nothing about the curve to the left of \(x\). Move \(x\) to \(-1\) with a large \(h\) and the chord runs off across the dip, giving a slope with the wrong sign entirely.

If asked how small \(h\) should be: not as small as you like. Below about \(10^{-8}\) the two values of \(f\) agree in so many leading digits that the subtraction keeps almost nothing, and the quotient becomes noise. Rule of thumb, \(h\approx10^{-8}\) in double precision, and if that is not accurate enough the answer is the next two slides or automatic differentiation.

The backward difference

Step back from \(x\).

$$\frac{df}{dx}\approx\frac{f(x)-f(x-h)}{h}$$

The same quotient, with the interval behind the point.

  • Same cost, same error size as forward — the error just leans the other way
  • For when \(f(x+h)\) cannot be had: the edge of a grid, the end of a time series, a constraint in the way

Speaker notes

Thirty seconds, and it exists mainly so the next slide is obvious. Nothing is new: the same two evaluations, the same division, the interval simply lies to the left. The error is again proportional to \(h\).

Put the two side by side at \(x=1.4\), \(h=1\). Forward read \(2.02\), too high, because the curve steepens to the right. Backward reads \(-0.08\), too low, because the curve is flatter — and at that distance, falling — to the left. The truth, \(0.72\), is between them. Say that plainly and then stop: the room has just invented the next slide.

When it is the only option: at the right-hand edge of a grid or the latest sample of a time series there is no \(f(x+h)\) to evaluate, and in an optimizer there may be a constraint immediately to the right. Then you step backwards and take the same error.

The central difference

Step both ways and split the difference.

$$\frac{df}{dx}\approx\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$$

The chord from \(x-h\) to \(x+h\). Note the \(2h\): the run is twice as long.

  • The average of the forward and backward quotients
  • Error proportional to \(h^2\): halve \(h\), quarter the error
  • Two evaluations, and \(f(x)\) itself is not one of them

The two one-sided errors lean opposite ways and cancel.

Speaker notes

The default, and the one to remember. Add the forward and backward quotients and divide by two: the \(f(x)\) terms cancel and what is left is this. The error terms also nearly cancel, because one was too high and the other too low by almost the same amount, and that is the whole reason this is better.

The numbers, at \(x=1.4\) with \(h=1\), against the true \(0.72\): forward \(2.02\), backward \(-0.08\), central \(0.97\). The two one-sided estimates were out by \(1.30\) and \(-0.80\); the central one is out by \(0.25\), with the same two function evaluations.

Then shrink \(h\) and let the room watch the difference in kind. From \(h=1\) to \(h=0.5\), forward's error roughly halves while central's drops by four. By \(h=0.1\) the chord and the dashed tangent are one line on screen and the error is in the third decimal. Error proportional to \(h^2\), and for this cubic it is exactly \(h^2/4\) if anyone checks.

The cost, said honestly: two evaluations either way, so central is free compared to forward — unless \(f(x)\) was already known for other reasons, which in an optimizer it usually is, and then forward costs one extra evaluation and central costs two.

Rule of thumb for \(h\): with central differences the sweet spot is around \(10^{-5}\) rather than \(10^{-8}\), because the rounding noise in the subtraction balances against a smaller truncation error. Below that it degrades the same way, for the same reason.

Forward-mode automatic differentiation

Every number carries its derivative.

$$\begin{aligned} (u,\dot u)+(v,\dot v) &= (u+v,\;\dot u+\dot v)\\ c\,(u,\dot u) &= (c\,u,\;c\,\dot u)\\ (u,\dot u)^n &= \bigl(u^n,\;n\,u^{n-1}\dot u\bigr)\\ g\bigl((u,\dot u)\bigr) &= \Bigl(g(u),\;\tfrac{dg}{du}\,\dot u\Bigr) \end{aligned}$$

\(\dot u\) is \(\frac{du}{dx}\), carried alongside \(u\).

Forward mode is the chain rule, applied one operation at a time.

x   = (2,  1)          # seed: dx/dx = 1
x²  = (4,  2·2·1)      # = (4, 4)
u   = x²/2 = (2,  2)
u²  = (4,  2·2·2)      # = (4, 8)
y   = u²/2 = (2,  4)

The derivative of \(y=\tfrac18x^4\) at \(x=2\) is \(\tfrac12x^3=4\). The program never saw that formula.

Speaker notes

Replace every number in the program by a pair: its value and its derivative with respect to the input. Then overload every operation so it computes both halves of the pair, using the rules of section I: the sum rule, the constant-multiple rule, the power rule, and — the last line — the chain rule, which covers every built-in function. Nothing new is being said — the rules are just being executed by the machine as it goes.

Walk the trace, which is the chain-rule example run as a program. Seed the input with derivative 1, because \(dx/dx=1\). Squaring gives \((4,\,4)\) by the power rule. Halving gives \((2,\,2)\) by the constant-multiple rule — that is \(u\) and \(\frac{du}{dx}\), and both halves are right. Squaring again gives \((4,\,8)\), and halving gives \((2,\,4)\).

So \(y=2\) and \(\frac{dy}{dx}=4\). Check it against the chain rule done by hand: \(u\cdot x=2\cdot2=4\), and against the symbolic route on the three-ways slide, \(\tfrac12x^3=4\). Three routes, one number — and no \(h\) to choose, which is the thing the last three slides were fighting.

A real implementation adds a rule per primitive — products, quotients, comparisons — and the whole thing is a page of code. These pairs are called dual numbers if anyone asks, and the dot notation is Newton's, kept here because it fits in a pair.

The cost of forward mode

  • One seed per pass: the derivative with respect to one input
  • A gradient of \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\) needs \(n\) passes
  • A network: millions of inputs, one output

Many inputs, one output: forward mode pays once per input. Reverse mode pays once per output.

Speaker notes

Look at what the seed did on the last slide: it chose which input the derivative was with respect to. To get \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\), seed \(x_1\) with 1 and everything else with 0, and run. To get \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\), run again. A gradient with \(n\) components is \(n\) runs of the program, each as expensive as evaluating \(f\) itself.

In terms of section III: one forward pass computes \(J\,d\) for one direction \(d\), which is a single column of the Jacobian. For a map with many inputs and few outputs the Jacobian is wide and short — one row, for a loss — and building it a column at a time is the wrong way round.

The alternative is to propagate derivatives backward from the output and get a whole row of \(J\) per pass: for a scalar loss, the entire gradient in one run, at a small constant multiple of the cost of \(f\). That is reverse mode, and it is what backpropagation is. It is the first thing lecture 4's training half builds. Nothing more on it today.

Speaker notes

Answer: A. \(f(1)=1\), \(\frac{df}{dx}=3x^2\) which is 3 at \(x=1\), and \(1+3\cdot0.1=1.3\). What is \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(1+x^2\bigr)^3\)?

  1. A\(3\bigl(1+x^2\bigr)^2\)
  2. B\(6x\bigl(1+x^2\bigr)^2\)
  3. C\(3(2x)^2\)
  4. D\(6x\bigl(1+x^2\bigr)^3\)

Speaker notes

Answer: B. Inner \(u=1+x^2\), outer \(y=u^3\). The outer rate is \(3u^2=3(1+x^2)^2\), the inner rate is \(2x\), and the product is \(6x(1+x^2)^2\). A is the outer rate alone — the inner rate forgotten, which is the commonest error of all. C evaluated the outer rate at the inner rate instead of the inner value, which is the trap the chain-rule slide named. D multiplied by the inner rate but never lowered the exponent.

If the room wants it checked: expanding gives \(1+3x^2+3x^4+x^6\), whose derivative is \(6x+12x^3+6x^5\), and \(6x(1+x^2)^2=6x(1+2x^2+x^4)\) is the same thing.

\(g:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2\) and \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^4\). The Jacobian of \(f\circ g\) is

  2. B\(3\times4\)

Speaker notes

Answer: A. \(f\circ g\) takes three numbers in and gives four out, so its Jacobian has four rows and three columns.
