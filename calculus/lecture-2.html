Rates of change and the difference quotient

How much \(f\) changes, per unit of \(x\).

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Rise over run, between \(x\) and \(x+h\).

  • An average rate over one interval
  • The slope of the line through two points on the curve
  • Depends on \(h\) — until \(h\) is small

Speaker notes

Speed is the example everyone has: distance travelled divided by time taken is an average speed, and the shorter the interval the closer it gets to what the speedometer says. The difference quotient is that, for any function.

Do one by hand, out loud, with \(f(x)=x^2\): \((x+h)^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2\), divide by \(h\), and the quotient is \(2x + h\). At \(x=1\): \(h=1\) gives 3, \(h=0.1\) gives 2.1, \(h=0.01\) gives 2.01. The \(h\) in the answer is the part that depends on the interval; it shrinks with the interval and leaves \(2x\).

"As \(h\) shrinks" is the whole of the limit in this lecture. No epsilon, no delta. The next slide puts it on a slider. If asked when it fails: a corner, like \(|x|\) at zero, has a different slope from each side, and no tangent.

The derivative as best local linear approximation

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h$$

\(f'(x)\) is the slope the secants settle on as \(h\to0\).

  • Of all lines through \((x, f(x))\), the one that fits best nearby
  • The error shrinks faster than \(h\)

Zoom in far enough and a smooth curve is a line. The derivative is its slope.

Speaker notes

The load-bearing slide of the hour. Pull \(h\) down slowly and narrate: the blue secant swings, and the number beside it settles. Somewhere around \(h=0.1\) it stops visibly moving. The green line it settles on is the tangent, and its slope is \(f'(x)\). Then move \(x\) and do it again somewhere the curve bends the other way.

Read the approximation as a recipe: to predict \(f\) a little way from where you are, take the value you have and add slope times step. That is a linear function of \(h\), which is why the next lecture can do anything at all — it replaces the function it cannot see with the line it can.

"Error shrinks faster than \(h\)" is the precise sense of best. Halve the step and the error drops by about four, not two. The quadratic-approximation slide says exactly what that error is.

Notation, said once: \(f'(x)\), \(\frac{df}{dx}\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}f\) all mean this number. The lecture uses the prime.

Differentiation rules

The ones this series uses.

$$\begin{aligned} (f+g)' &= f'+g' &\qquad (c\,f)' &= c\,f'\\ (x^n)' &= n\,x^{n-1} &\qquad (e^x)' &= e^x \end{aligned}$$
  • Each is the linear approximation, worked out once
  • Together they differentiate every polynomial

Speaker notes

Not a slide to dwell on. The sum rule and constant rule say differentiation is itself linear, in last week's sense: it respects addition and scaling. Point at the figure for the power rule — the slope of the parabola at each \(x\) is the height of the line, and the two cross zero together.

The power rule was done on the previous slide for \(n=2\): the quotient was \(2x+h\), and the \(h\) fell away. For general \(n\), expand \((x+h)^n\); the term linear in \(h\) is \(n x^{n-1} h\), and every other term carries at least \(h^2\). The exponential is the function that is its own slope — accept it as a fact today.

Product and quotient rules exist and are in every textbook. Nothing in the next three lectures differentiates a product of two unknown functions, so they are not taught here. Sine and cosine likewise. What the series does differentiate, constantly, is a function of a function — the next slide.

The chain rule

The derivative of a function of a function.

$$\bigl(f(g(x))\bigr)' = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x)$$

Outer rate, at the inner value, times inner rate.

  • \(x\) moves \(u=g(x)\) at rate \(g'\)
  • \(u\) moves \(y=f(u)\) at rate \(f'\)

Rates compose by multiplying.

Speaker notes

The second load-bearing slide, and the one lecture 4 stands on entirely. The figure is \(g(x)=x^2\), \(f(u)=\sin u\), so \(y=\sin(x^2)\). A small step in \(x\) (red, top line) lands as a step in \(u\) that is \(g'(x)=2x\) times as long, and that lands as a step in \(y\) that is \(f'(u)=\cos u\) times as long again. Two stretches in a row: multiply.

Read the numbers off the slider at \(x=1\): \(u=1\), \(g'=2\), \(f'=\cos 1\approx0.54\), so \(dy/dx\approx1.08\). Slide \(x\) toward \(1.25\): \(u=1.5625\), \(\cos u\) is small, and the bottom step almost vanishes though the middle one is wider than before. Around \(x=1.77\), \(u\approx\pi\) and the bottom step reverses.

The trap, worth naming: \(f'\) is evaluated at \(g(x)\), not at \(x\). It is the rate of the outer function at the value it actually receives.

Forward reference, said plainly: a neural network is a chain of a dozen functions, and its derivative is a dozen factors multiplied. Lecture 4 is about the order to multiply them in.

Stationary points

Where the tangent is flat.

$$f'(x)=0$$
  • Every smooth minimum or maximum is one
  • Not every one is a minimum or maximum
  • What an optimizer looks for

Speaker notes

If the slope at a smooth minimum were nonzero, the linear approximation would say: step a little against the slope and go lower. So it cannot be a minimum. That is the entire argument, and it runs the same way for a maximum.

The converse fails. \(x^3\) is flat at zero and keeps rising; a flat tangent alone does not say which of the three cases you are in. The next slide says how to tell.

The figure is \(f(x)=\tfrac14(x^3-3x)+1\), so \(f'(x)=\tfrac34(x^2-1)\), zero at \(x=\pm1\). Values: \(f(-1)=1.5\), \(f(1)=0.5\).

The third bullet is the coordination point with lecture 3: it will not define a minimum by this condition, it will build algorithms and find that this is where they stop. "Local" is the word to plant now — these are the lowest points nearby, and nothing here says anything about the whole line.

The second derivative

The rate at which the slope changes.

$$f''=(f')'$$
  • \(f''>0\): slope increasing, curve bends upward
  • \(f''<0\): slope decreasing, curve bends downward
  • At a stationary point: \(f''>0\) means a minimum, \(f''<0\) a maximum

Speaker notes

Differentiate again. For the running curve, \(f'=\tfrac34(x^2-1)\), so \(f''=\tfrac32 x\): negative on the left, positive on the right, zero at the origin where the bend changes direction.

At \(x=-1\), \(f''=-1.5\): the slope is falling through zero, from positive to negative, which is what a hilltop is. At \(x=1\), \(f''=1.5\): the slope rises through zero, a valley floor. That is the second-derivative test, and it is nothing more than reading which way the slope is going.

Say the word curvature once. The larger \(|f''|\), the tighter the bend, the sooner the tangent line stops being a good approximation. The next slide fixes that.

If \(f''=0\) at a stationary point the test is silent — \(x^3\) again. Name it and move on.

The quadratic approximation

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\tfrac12 f''(x)\,h^2$$

The tangent line, plus the curvature.

  • Error now shrinks like \(h^3\)
  • A parabola: the simplest thing with a minimum

Add curvature to the line and the fit survives a step further out.

Speaker notes

Blue is the tangent from the linear-approximation slide; green is the same thing with one more term. Slide \(x\) about. Near the point the two are indistinguishable from the curve. A step away, the line has left and the parabola is still there. Where the curve is nearly straight, near \(x=0\), the parabola flattens into the line; where it bends hard, the parabola bends with it.

The half is not decoration. Differentiate the right-hand side twice in \(h\) and you get \(f''(x)\), as you must. The linear term was already \(f'(x)h\), and the quadratic term is chosen so the second derivatives agree too.

This is as far as the lecture expands. Taylor's theorem continues the pattern — \(\tfrac16 f''' h^3\) and on — and the series does not need it. What lecture 3 needs is exactly this: Newton's method replaces \(f\) by this parabola and jumps to the parabola's minimum. Where the green curve bottoms out is where Newton would step to.

Functions of several variables

A vector in, a number out.

$$f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R,\qquad f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2$$
  • A surface over the plane, or
  • Its contour lines: where \(f\) is constant
  • Close contours mean steep ground

Speaker notes

Everything in section I had one number going in. From here a vector goes in and a number comes out — a loss, a cost, a height. The picture is a landscape, and the lecture draws it from above, as a map with contour lines, because that is the picture that survives into more than two dimensions in the mind's eye.

The running example is a bowl, stretched: \(x^2+2y^2\). The contours are ellipses, twice as tight in \(y\) as in \(x\), because \(y\) is charged double. Walking in \(y\) climbs faster. This same bowl is the example lecture 3 uses for steepest descent, so it is worth getting familiar with.

The point at \((1.5, 0.8)\): \(2.25 + 2\cdot0.64 = 3.53\). Every figure in this section stands at that point.

Partial derivatives

Vary one input, hold the others still.

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,\,y)-f(x,\,y)}{h}$$

An ordinary derivative along one axis.

  • One per input
  • For \(x^2+2y^2\): \(\;\partial f/\partial x=2x,\quad\partial f/\partial y=4y\)

Speaker notes

Cut the landscape with a vertical plane along the \(x\) direction. The cut is an ordinary curve, and it has an ordinary slope: that is \(\partial f/\partial x\). Cut along \(y\) and get the other one. The curly \(\partial\) is a reminder that something is being held fixed; the arithmetic is section I with \(y\) treated as a constant.

At \((1.5, 0.8)\): the \(x\)-slice is \(x^2+1.28\), slope \(2x=3\). The \(y\)-slice is \(2.25+2y^2\), slope \(4y=3.2\). The right-hand panel shows both slices with their tangents; the \(y\) one is the steeper.

Two numbers, one for each axis. The next slide puts them together into one arrow — and then says that the arrow, not the pair of numbers, is the real object, which is exactly the move last week made with vectors and coordinates.

The gradient

$$\nabla f=\begin{pmatrix}\partial f/\partial x\\ \partial f/\partial y\end{pmatrix}$$

Its direction: steepest ascent. Its length: how steep.

  • Perpendicular to the contour through the point
  • \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\nabla f\cdot h\) — the linear approximation again

The gradient points straight uphill.

Speaker notes

The third load-bearing slide, and the object lecture 3 is built from. Drag the point around and say what does not change: the red arrow always crosses the contour at a right angle, always points to higher ground, and gets longer where the contours crowd together.

Definition first, geometrically: among all directions to step, one climbs fastest, and the gradient is the arrow in that direction whose length is that fastest rate. Coordinates second: in a Cartesian frame, its components are the partial derivatives. That is a theorem, not a definition, and the reason is the linear approximation. Near the point, \(f\) is a plane, \(f(x)+\nabla f\cdot h\). Along the contour that plane is flat, so the contour direction has zero inner product with \(\nabla f\) — perpendicular, in last week's sense. And the inner product is largest when \(h\) points along \(\nabla f\).

Drag to \((1.5, 0.8)\): \(\nabla f=(3, 3.2)\), length \(\sqrt{9+10.24}\approx4.39\). Note it does not point at the origin, where the minimum is: it points at the steepest way up here. The bowl is not round, and that gap between "steepest" and "toward the bottom" is the zig-zag lecture 3 spends a slide on.

The arrow is drawn at a third of its true length so it fits the figure.

Directional derivatives

The rate of climb in a chosen direction.

$$D_d f=\nabla f\cdot d,\qquad \lVert d\rVert=1$$
  • Largest along \(\nabla f\), zero along the contour
  • Negative: that way is downhill

Every direction's slope is one inner product with the gradient.

Speaker notes

The gradient is one arrow, but you may want to walk any way you please. The slope in that direction is the inner product of the direction with the gradient — the inner-product slide from last week, doing its first real job. Turn the blue arrow with the slider and watch the number.

At \(46.8^\circ\) it is aligned with \(\nabla f\) and reads \(4.39\), the full length. Ninety degrees off, at about \(137^\circ\) or \(317^\circ\), it reads zero: that is walking along the contour, neither up nor down. Past that it goes negative, and at \(227^\circ\), straight against the gradient, it reads \(-4.39\): the fastest way down.

The formula is the linear approximation with \(h=t\,d\): \(f(x+td)\approx f(x) + t\,\nabla f\cdot d\), so the rate per unit \(t\) is \(\nabla f\cdot d\). With \(\lVert d\rVert=1\) this is \(\lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta\).

Forward reference to plant: lecture 3 calls any \(d\) with a negative reading a descent direction, and every method in that lecture is "pick one, step along it".

The Jacobian

A vector in, a vector out.

$$J_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j},\qquad f(x+h)\approx f(x)+J\,h$$

Row \(i\) is the gradient of output \(i\). Column \(j\) is where a step in \(x_j\) goes.

Locally, every smooth map is a linear map: its Jacobian.

Speaker notes

Last week a matrix was a map of the plane and its columns were where the basis vectors went. A nonlinear map bends the grid — the right-hand panel. But look at the small square: it has come out as a parallelogram, to the eye. A linear map did that, and the linear map is the Jacobian, the matrix of all the partial derivatives at that point. Its columns are where small steps along each axis land.

The figure's map is \((x,y)\mapsto(x+\tfrac14y^2,\;y+\tfrac14x^2)\). At \(p=(1,1)\): \(\partial f_1/\partial x=1\), \(\partial f_1/\partial y=y/2=0.5\), \(\partial f_2/\partial x=x/2=0.5\), \(\partial f_2/\partial y=1\). So \(J\) is the two-by-two with ones on the diagonal and halves off it — a shear both ways. The dashed green parallelogram is \(J\) applied to the square; the red curve is the true image. They differ by less than the line width.

Shapes: \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\) gives an \(m\times n\) Jacobian. A single output makes it one row, the gradient lying down. Lecture 3 uses the Jacobian of a residual vector for Gauss–Newton; lecture 4 has one per layer.

The Hessian

All the second derivatives, in a matrix.

$$H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j},\qquad f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\nabla f\cdot h+\tfrac12\,h^{\mathsf T}H\,h$$
  • Symmetric: the order of the two derivatives does not matter
  • For \(x^2+2y^2\): \(H=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\), everywhere

The Hessian is the curvature of the bowl, in every direction at once.

Speaker notes

The quadratic-approximation slide, with a vector \(h\). The linear term is the gradient against the step, as on the gradient slide; the new term is a quadratic form: step, Hessian, step. For a direction \(d\) of length one, \(d^{\mathsf T}Hd\) is the second derivative of \(f\) along \(d\) — the curvature of the slice in that direction.

For the bowl: \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2=2\), \(\partial^2 f/\partial y^2=4\), the mixed one zero. Along \(x\) the curvature is 2, along \(y\) it is 4, and the contours are correspondingly squashed. Those two numbers are the eigenvalues of \(H\), from last week's eigenvalues slide, and the two arrows are its eigenvectors. For a general bowl they would not lie along the axes, but they would still be the tight and loose directions.

Because this \(f\) is itself quadratic, the approximation here is exact — worth saying, since it is the reason a figure of the model would just redraw the figure of the function.

Two forward references. Newton's method in lecture 3 minimizes this quadratic directly, which needs the Hessian. And the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue — here 2 — is the condition number, which decides how badly steepest descent zig-zags.

The chain rule in several variables

$$J_{f\circ g}(x)=J_f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\;J_g(x)$$

Outer Jacobian, at the inner value, times inner Jacobian.

  • Same rule, with matrices in place of numbers
  • The shapes must agree — and they do

Rates compose by matrix multiplication.

Speaker notes

Chain rule, second time. A small step \(h\) in \(x\) becomes \(J_g h\) in the middle space, by the Jacobian slide. That step becomes \(J_f(J_g h)\) in the last space. Two linear maps applied in turn are one linear map, their product — the matrix-product slide from last week — so the Jacobian of the composition is the product of the Jacobians. In one variable the matrices are \(1\times1\) and this is the chain rule of section I.

The shapes on the figure: \(J_g\) is \(3\times2\), \(J_f\) is \(4\times3\), and \(4\times3\) times \(3\times2\) is \(4\times2\), which is what a map from \(\mathbb R^2\) to \(\mathbb R^4\) must have. Order matters: \(J_gJ_f\) does not even have compatible shapes.

This slide is backpropagation, stated. A network is \(f_L\circ\dots\circ f_1\), its derivative is \(J_L\cdots J_1\), and lecture 4 is about the fact that a product of matrices can be evaluated left-to-right or right-to-left, and that the cost differs enormously. Section IV starts on why.

By hand, by difference quotient, by program

Three ways to get the number \(f'(x)\).

Symbolic

d/dx sin(x²)
  = cos(x²) · 2x

Exact. Done once, by a person or a computer algebra system.

Numerical

(f(x + h) − f(x)) / h

Any \(f\) you can evaluate. Approximate, and the choice of \(h\) matters.

Automatic

x = (2, 1)
u = x·x
y = sin(u)

Exact, to rounding. The program computes \(f'\) alongside \(f\).

Speaker notes

Everything so far says what a derivative is. A program needs the number. Three ways.

Symbolic is what section I did: apply the rules, get a formula, type it in. Exact, but somebody has to do it for every function, and for a function of a million parameters written as code nobody will.

Numerical is the difference quotient with a finite \(h\), and it works on any black box you can call. It is approximate, and the next slide is about how approximate.

Automatic differentiation is the one most people have not met. The program that computes \(f\) is run as written, but every number carries its derivative along with it, and every operation updates both by the rules of section I. No formula is ever produced and nothing is approximated. Two slides on it follow.

Numerical differentiation and the step size

$$\text{error}\;\approx\;\underbrace{\tfrac{h}{2}\,|f''|}_{\text{truncation}} \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon\,|f|}{h}}_{\text{cancellation}}$$
  • Large \(h\): the secant is not the tangent
  • Small \(h\): \(f(x+h)-f(x)\) loses its digits
  • Best near \(h\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon}\approx10^{-8}\)

Speaker notes

The plot is computed live, in double precision, for \(f(x)=e^x\) at \(x=1\), where the true derivative is \(e\). Red is the plain forward difference; green is the central difference \((f(x+h)-f(x-h))/2h\). Both axes are logarithmic.

Slide \(h\) from the right. At \(h=10^{-1}\) the error is about \(0.14\): the secant is visibly not the tangent, and the quadratic-approximation slide says the error is about \(\tfrac{h}{2}f''\), which here is \(1.36h\). Each decade down in \(h\) buys a decade of accuracy, as it should — until about \(10^{-8}\). Then it turns round.

What went wrong is subtraction. \(f(x+h)\) and \(f(x)\) agree in their leading digits, the difference keeps only the trailing ones, and dividing by a tiny \(h\) magnifies whatever rounding noise was in them: about \(\varepsilon|f|/h\) with \(\varepsilon\approx2\cdot10^{-16}\). By \(h=10^{-16}\), \(x+h\) rounds to \(x\) and the quotient is exactly zero. The two errors balance near \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\), and the best you get is about eight digits of sixteen.

Central difference cancels the \(h\) term; its truncation error is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''\), so it reaches about \(10^{-11}\) near \(h=10^{-5}\). Better, still not exact, and two evaluations instead of one.

Forward-mode automatic differentiation

Every number carries its derivative.

$$\begin{aligned} (u,u')+(v,v') &= (u+v,\;u'+v')\\ (u,u')^n &= (u^n,\;n\,u^{n-1}u')\\ g\bigl((u,u')\bigr) &= \bigl(g(u),\;g'(u)\,u'\bigr) \end{aligned}$$

Forward mode is the chain rule, applied one operation at a time.

x = (2,      1)        # seed: dx/dx = 1
u = x²  = (4,      2·2·1)  # = (4, 4)
y = sin(u) = (sin 4, cos 4 · 4)
         = (−0.757, −2.614)

The derivative of \(\sin(x^2)\) at \(x=2\) is \(4\cos4\approx-2.614\). The program never saw that formula.

Speaker notes

Replace every number in the program by a pair: its value and its derivative with respect to the input. Then overload every operation so it computes both halves of the pair, using the rules of section I. The sum rule gives the first line; the power rule the second; the third is the chain rule and covers every built-in function. Nothing new is being said — the rules are just being executed by the machine as it goes.

Walk the trace. Seed the input with derivative 1, because \(dx/dx=1\). Squaring gives value 4 and derivative \(2\cdot2\cdot1=4\). Sine gives value \(\sin4\approx -0.757\) and derivative \(\cos4\cdot4\approx-0.654\cdot4=-2.614\). Check against the symbolic answer from two slides ago: \(2x\cos(x^2)\) at 2 is \(4\cos4\). Same number, to rounding, with no formula in sight.

A real implementation adds a rule per primitive — products, quotients, division, comparisons — and the whole thing is a page of code. These pairs are called dual numbers if anyone asks.

The cost of forward mode

  • One seed per pass: the derivative with respect to one input
  • A gradient of \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\) needs \(n\) passes
  • A network: millions of inputs, one output

Many inputs, one output: forward mode pays once per input. Reverse mode pays once per output.

Speaker notes

Look at what the seed did on the last slide: it chose which input the derivative was with respect to. To get \(\partial f/\partial x_1\) seed \(x_1\) with 1 and everything else with 0; run. To get \(\partial f/\partial x_2\), run again. A gradient with \(n\) components is \(n\) runs of the program, each as expensive as evaluating \(f\) itself.

In terms of the last section: one forward pass computes \(Jd\) for one direction \(d\), a single column of the Jacobian. For a map with many inputs and few outputs the Jacobian is wide and short — one row, for a loss — and building it a column at a time is the wrong way round.

The alternative is to propagate derivatives backward from the output, and get a whole row of \(J\) per pass: for a scalar loss, the entire gradient in one run, at a small constant multiple of the cost of \(f\). That is reverse mode, and it is what backpropagation is. It is the first thing lecture 4's training half builds. Nothing more on it today.

Cut candidate: if short, say the first bullet on the previous slide and skip this one.

Quiz

Four questions, hands up.

Speaker notes

Multiple choice, answered from the room. Read each one out, count hands for each letter, then say the answer. If time is short, ask 1 and 3: the linear approximation and the gradient are what lecture 3 leans on hardest.

The tangent to \(f(x)=x^3\) at \(x=1\) predicts \(f(1.1)\) as

  1. A\(1.3\)
  2. B\(1.331\)
  3. C\(1.03\)
  4. D\(3.3\)

Speaker notes

Answer: A. \(f(1)=1\), \(f'(x)=3x^2\) so \(f'(1)=3\), and \(1+3\cdot0.1=1.3\). B is the exact value \(1.1^3\) — the tangent under-predicts, by \(0.031\), and that gap is the \(\tfrac12f''h^2\) term: \(f''(1)=6\), \(3\cdot0.01=0.03\), nearly all of it. C forgot to multiply by the slope; D forgot to add the value at the point.

What is the derivative of \(\sin(x^2)\)?

  1. A\(\cos(x^2)\)
  2. B\(2x\cos(x^2)\)
  3. C\(2x\cos(2x)\)
  4. D\(\cos(x^2)\cdot\sin(2x)\)

Speaker notes

Answer: B. Outer rate \(\cos\) at the inner value \(x^2\), times the inner rate \(2x\). A forgot the inner rate. C evaluated the outer rate at the wrong place — at \(g'(x)\) instead of \(g(x)\) — which is the trap the chain-rule slide named. D differentiated both and multiplied, which is not a rule.

At a point on a contour line of \(f\), the gradient \(\nabla f\) is

  1. AAlong the contour
  2. BPerpendicular to the contour, pointing uphill
  3. CPerpendicular to the contour, pointing downhill
  4. DPointing at the minimum of \(f\)

Speaker notes

Answer: B. Along the contour \(f\) does not change, so the directional derivative there is zero, so the gradient has zero inner product with the contour direction. It points up because that is how it is defined; down is minus the gradient, which lecture 3 will walk along. D is the good trap: it is true only when the contours are circles. On the stretched bowl the arrow at \((1.5, 0.8)\) pointed well away from the origin, and if the room picked D, go back to the gradient slide and drag.

\(g:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2\) and \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^4\). The Jacobian of \(f\circ g\) is

  1. A\(4\times3\)
  2. B\(3\times4\)
  3. C\(2\times2\)
  4. D\(4\times2\)

Answer: A. \(f\circ g\) takes three numbers in and gives four out, so its Jacobian has four rows and three columns.
