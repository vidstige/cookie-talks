The minimization problem

Find the input that makes the output smallest.

$$x^\star=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\;f(x)$$

The place, not the value.

  • Maximizing \(f\) is minimizing \(-f\)
  • Here \(f(x)=\tfrac14x^3-\tfrac34x+1\), on \([-0.4,\,2.6]\)

An optimizer never sees the function — only the values it asks for.

Speaker notes

The same curve as last week, given a job. Over this stretch it falls and then rises, and the bottom sits at \(x=1\) with \(f(1)=\tfrac14-\tfrac34+1=0.5\).

Separate the two things the figure marks, because they are confused constantly. The minimum is \(0.5\), a value on the vertical axis. The minimizer is \(1\), a place on the horizontal axis. Optimization wants the place. In lecture 4 the value will be a loss nobody cares about and the place will be the weights of a network, which is the whole product.

The claim is the rule for the next three slides, and it is worth dwelling on. The algorithm is handed a black box: it may name an \(x\) and be told \(f(x)\), and nothing else. It cannot see the shape on the screen. Every method in the hour is a strategy for choosing which \(x\) to ask about next.

Maximizing is the same problem upside down, so nothing is lost by only ever minimizing. Say it once and never mention maxima again until the landscape.

Load-bearing: the distinction between the minimum and the minimizer is used on every slide after this one.

Bracketing a minimum

Three points that trap it.

$$a<b<c,\qquad f(b)<f(a),\quad f(b)<f(c)$$

Then \(f\) has a minimum somewhere in \((a,\,c)\).

  • Two comparisons, no arithmetic
  • Going down and then up again has to turn somewhere

Speaker notes

Slide \(b\) and read the shading out loud. Over most of the range \(f(b)\) is below both ends and the shading is there: a minimum is trapped, and no further evaluation can lose it. Push \(b\) out past about \(1.9\) and the shading goes, because \(f(b)\) has climbed back above \(f(a)\). Three points on a curve that simply rises look exactly like that, and nothing is trapped.

Why it works, in one sentence: the curve gets from \(f(a)\) down to \(f(b)\) and back up to \(f(c)\), so it turns around inside. That is the whole argument, and it needs continuity and nothing else — no derivative, no formula, not even arithmetic beyond comparing two numbers.

Name the assumption honestly while the slider is in hand: this finds a minimum in the bracket, not the lowest point of the function. If the curve wiggled several times between \(a\) and \(c\) the bracket would still be valid and would still trap only one of the dips. That is the first appearance of a problem the landscape section returns to.

Everything that follows in this section keeps the bracket property true and makes the bracket narrower. That is the entire design.

Halving the bracket

Two probes, one either side of the middle.

  • Compare \(f\) at \(m-\varepsilon\) and \(m+\varepsilon\)
  • Keep the side holding the smaller one
  • Two evaluations bought half the width
$$\sqrt{0.5}\approx0.707\ \text{per evaluation}$$

Speaker notes

Walk the slider out one step at a time and say what each step does. Two fresh points are placed a hair apart around the middle of the bracket. Whichever is lower, the minimum is on that side, because the curve is already rising by the time it reaches the other probe. Throw the far half away and repeat.

The readout carries the argument: the width goes 3, 1.62, 0.875, 0.47, while the evaluation count goes 2, 4, 6, 8. It is a little over half each time rather than exactly half, and the excess is the gap between the probes, which shrinks along with the bracket. Say the comparison out loud in the form that matters — not per step, but per evaluation, because evaluations are what a real objective charges for. Half the width for two evaluations is \(\sqrt{0.5}\), about \(0.707\), each time we ask.

Then name the limit. Every step cuts the bracket by the same factor, so each correct digit costs the same number of evaluations, about seven, and shrinking the bracket a thousandfold takes twenty. Placing the probes more cleverly trims that constant but leaves the rate alone. The next section changes the rate, by asking for the slope.

If asked about \(\varepsilon\): it has to be small enough to be inside the bracket and large enough that the two values differ in floating point. The figure exaggerates it so the two probes can be told apart.

Cut candidate: if the hour is running long, state the halving rate in a sentence and move on to the derivative.

Where the derivative vanishes

The first thing a slope says about a lowest point.

$$\frac{df}{dx}(x^\star)=0$$

True at every minimum. Also true elsewhere.

$$\tfrac34\bigl(x^2-1\bigr)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;x=-1,\ x=1$$

Zero slope is a clue, not a proof.

Speaker notes

The argument takes one sentence and is worth saying slowly. If the slope at a point is not zero, then one of the two directions goes downhill, so a small step that way lowers \(f\), so the point was not the lowest. Only a zero slope survives.

Drive the marker across and read the lower panel: left of \(-1\) the slope is positive, between \(-1\) and \(1\) it is negative, right of \(1\) positive again. It crosses zero twice. The derivative is \(\tfrac34(x^2-1)\), which vanishes at \(x=\pm1\), and that is arithmetic anyone can do.

Now the claim, which is why the lecture does not end here. At \(x=1\) the curve has its lowest point. At \(x=-1\) it has a peak, and the slope is every bit as zero. Solving \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\) does not distinguish them. Say that the fix is the second derivative and leave it there for now; it comes back with a name attached.

Two honest limits. Solving \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\) by hand only works when the derivative is something we can solve, which it usually is not. And with a vector going in it becomes a system of equations, not one equation. Both are reasons to search rather than solve.

Load-bearing: Newton's method is built directly on this slide, and so are the normal equations.

Descent directions

Which way is downhill, when there are many ways to go.

$$\nabla f\,d<0$$

A row times a column: the rate of change of \(f\) along \(d\), called the directional derivative.

  • Zero along the level curve — neither up nor down
  • Most negative opposite the gradient

A descent method picks a direction, then a distance.

Speaker notes

The rings are the level curves of \(x^2+2y^2\) seen from above — last week's bowl, flattened onto the page. Make that switch explicitly, because every figure from here to the end of the lecture is drawn this way.

The gradient at \((1.5,\,0.8)\) is the row \((3,\;3.2)\), and the number in the readout is \(3d_1+3.2d_2\). Sweep the angle all the way round and narrate it: over half the circle the number is negative and \(f\) falls, over the other half it rises, and at exactly two angles it is zero. Those two are the directions along the ring, where a small step changes nothing — the fact the gradient sits at right angles to its level curve, from lecture 2, now visible as a number passing through zero.

Any direction in the falling half will do, and that freedom is what the rest of the lecture spends. The most negative is \(d=-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\), which is the obvious choice and, as section III shows, often a poor one.

Worth saying once: this is the linear approximation from lecture 2 doing its job. \(f(x+\alpha d)\approx f(x)+\alpha\,\nabla f\,d\), so for small enough \(\alpha\) the sign of \(\nabla f\,d\) decides the sign of the change. The promise is only for small \(\alpha\), and that is exactly what the next slide has to deal with.

Line search

Along a fixed direction, one number goes in and one comes out.

$$\varphi(\alpha)=f(x+\alpha d)$$

Any method of the last section can minimize this.

$$\varphi(\alpha)\le\varphi(0) +c\,\alpha\,\frac{d\varphi}{d\alpha}(0)$$
  • Halve \(\alpha\) until it holds
  • \(c=\tfrac14\): bank a quarter of the promised drop

Speaker notes

Fix the direction and the several-variable problem collapses to a one-dimensional one. The curve on screen is \(f\) along the ray from \((1.5,\,0.8)\) in the steepest-descent direction; here it works out to \(3.53-19.24\alpha+29.48\alpha^2\), a parabola whose bottom sits at \(\alpha\approx0.326\), taking \(f\) from \(3.53\) down to \(0.39\).

But finding that bottom exactly costs evaluations, and it is not worth it: the direction was a guess, so the perfect distance along a guessed direction is wasted effort. What is wanted is a step that is cheap to find and definitely helps.

That is the second line, the sufficient-decrease or Armijo condition. The straight line on the figure starts at \(\varphi(0)\) and falls with a quarter of the initial slope. A trial \(\alpha\) is accepted when the curve is under that line. Read the trials out: at \(\alpha=1\) the curve is far above it, at \(\tfrac12\) still above, at \(\tfrac14\) below — accepted, three evaluations.

Why a fraction of the slope and not simply "any decrease": a rule that accepts any decrease at all can be satisfied by an endless sequence of ever tinier steps that converge somewhere harmless. Demanding a fixed share of what the slope promised rules that out.

Almost every serious method is this: a rule for the direction, and a backtracking line search for the distance. Say that, because sections III and IV are then mostly about the direction.

The quadratic approximation

A parabola instead of a line.

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\frac{df}{dx}h +\tfrac12\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}h^2$$

The line, plus the bend.

  • A line has no lowest point; a parabola has one
  • It opens upward only where \(\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}>0\)

Speaker notes

Lecture 2 replaced a curve by its tangent line. This is the same move with one more term, and the extra term is where the second derivative earns its keep.

Drag the point and compare the two dashed curves against the solid one. The line matches the height and the slope; the parabola matches the height, the slope and the bend, and it hugs the curve over a stretch where the line has long since left. For a cubic the model is wrong only by the cubic part, which is why it looks this good.

Now the point of the slide, and say it while dragging. Minimizing a line is a silly question — it runs off to infinity. Minimizing a parabola is a question with an answer, and the answer is a formula. So if a model must be minimized, the quadratic is the first one that is any use at all.

Push the point to the left of zero and stop there. The bend goes negative, the model turns upside down, and its stationary point is a peak. Any method that jumps to the model's stationary point will happily jump uphill here. Leave that hanging for exactly one slide.

Load-bearing: this is the model behind Newton's method in one dimension and in several, and behind Gauss–Newton.

Newton's method in one dimension

Jump to the bottom of the parabola, and fit a new one there.

$$x\;\leftarrow\;x-\frac{df/dx}{d^2f/dx^2}$$

The step that makes the model's slope zero.

  • From \(2.5\): \(1.45\), \(1.070\), \(1.0023\), \(1.0000026\)
  • The correct digits double each step

It finds where the slope is zero. It does not ask which way the curve bends.

Speaker notes

Fit the parabola of the previous slide, then go straight to its lowest point. Setting the model's slope to zero gives \(\frac{df}{dx}+\frac{d^2f}{dx^2}h=0\), so \(h=-\frac{df/dx}{d^2f/dx^2}\). One line of algebra, and it is the whole method.

Step the slider from \(2.5\) and read the numbers out. The errors are \(1.5\), \(0.45\), \(0.070\), \(0.0023\), \(0.0000026\): each is roughly the square of the one before. That is what doubling the correct digits means, and it is why Newton's method is the standard against which everything else is measured.

Now drag the start to the left of zero and let the room watch it converge happily to \(x=-1\), which is the peak. The bend there is negative, the parabola opens downward, and the method jumps to its top with no complaint. Nothing has gone wrong in the arithmetic; the method simply solves \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\) and that equation does not care.

A footnote for anyone who wants it: the iteration here simplifies to \(x\leftarrow(x^2+1)/(2x)\), which is the ancient method for extracting a square root, converging to \(\sqrt1\). The two are the same algorithm.

The practical fix, named now and used in section IV: when the bend is wrong or the step is wild, shorten it — a line search along the Newton direction, or a damping term.

Steepest descent

Take the direction the gradient forbids most strongly.

$$x\;\leftarrow\;x-\alpha\,\nabla f^{\mathsf T}$$

Transposed: the gradient is a row, the point is a column.

  • Needs one gradient per step and nothing else
  • Each step lands where the ring it started on is steepest

Steepest at a point is not quickest to the bottom.

Speaker notes

The obvious method, and the one everything in machine learning is descended from. Stand somewhere, ask the gradient which way is up, walk the other way, repeat.

Step it out and point at the right angles: every step leaves its ring perpendicular, because that is what the gradient does. With a fixed \(\alpha\) the steps also shrink as the path goes in, without anyone arranging it — the gradient itself gets smaller near the bottom. That is a real convenience and worth naming.

The claim is the seed of the next three slides. Steepest is a statement about an infinitesimal step at one point. It says nothing about where the bottom is, and on a stretched bowl the two directions disagree badly. This figure is mild; the zig-zag slide turns the stretch up.

On the transpose, briefly, since lecture 2 made the choice deliberately: \(\nabla f\) is a row so that \(\nabla f\,h\) is a matrix times a step. To move a point we need a column, so it gets transposed. Anyone writing gradients as columns will not see the transpose in their books; nothing else changes.

Choosing a step length

One number between crawling and blowing up.

  • Too small: many steps, little progress
  • Too large: the value climbs, and keeps climbing
  • On \(x^2+2y^2\), any \(\alpha\ge\tfrac12\) fails to converge
$$y\;\leftarrow\;(1-4\alpha)\,y$$

Speaker notes

Sweep \(\alpha\) slowly from the left and narrate three regimes. Small: the path is a tidy curve that has barely started after twelve steps. Around a third: it drops almost straight in. Past a half: the path throws itself across the bowl and each throw is bigger than the last.

The algebra is one line, and the figure is its picture. On this bowl the update is \(x\leftarrow(1-2\alpha)x\) and \(y\leftarrow(1-4\alpha)y\), so each coordinate is multiplied by a fixed number every step. Convergence needs both multipliers inside \((-1,1)\), and the \(y\) one leaves at \(\alpha=\tfrac12\). The threshold is set by the largest bend, which is \(4\): the rule is \(\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\).

Note which coordinate breaks first, because it is the point of the next slide. The steep direction blows up while the shallow one is still crawling. A single \(\alpha\) has to keep the steepest direction stable, and that ceiling is then imposed on every other direction too.

Where this shows up later: in lecture 4 this number is called the learning rate, it is the first thing anyone tunes, and the failure on the right of this slider is exactly what a diverging loss curve looks like.

The zig-zag

A narrow valley gets crossed rather than followed.

$$\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}},\qquad \text{per step}\;\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$$

Eigenvalues of the Hessian: the stiffest bend over the slackest.

  • \(\kappa=1\): one step. \(\kappa=100\): \(0.980\) of the error left
  • Even the best fixed \(\alpha\) zig-zags

The gradient points across the valley, not along it.

Speaker notes

Lecture 2 promised this slide on the Hessian, so pay it off by name. The condition number is the ratio of the largest bend to the smallest, and the eigenvalues of the Hessian are those bends — lecture 1's eigenvalues, doing something useful.

Turn the stretch up slowly. At 1 the rings are circles, the gradient points straight at the centre, and one step is enough. As the rings flatten the path starts to bounce, and by 20 it is crossing the valley almost at right angles, making almost no progress along it.

The figure uses the best fixed step there is, \(\alpha=2/(\lambda_{\min}+ \lambda_{\max})\), and that matters: this is not a badly tuned method, it is a well-tuned one showing its ceiling. With that step both coordinates are multiplied by \(\pm(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\) each time, and the sign flip on the stiff coordinate is the zig-zag. For \(\kappa=20\) the factor is \(0.905\), so a hundredfold reduction needs about forty-six steps.

Why this is not a curiosity: real problems are far worse than 20. The least-squares fit later in the hour has \(\kappa\approx63\) as written, and the landscapes in lecture 4 are worse again. Every method on the next three slides is an attempt to stop paying this price.

Load-bearing: momentum, per-coordinate steps and Newton's method are all answers to this figure.

Momentum

Keep some of the last step.

$$v\leftarrow\beta v-\alpha\nabla f^{\mathsf T},\qquad x\leftarrow x+v$$

A velocity that remembers, rather than a step that forgets.

  • Crossing parts cancel, downhill parts add up
  • \(\beta=0\) is plain descent; close to 1 it overshoots

Speaker notes

The zig-zag wastes work in a very specific way: the across-the-valley part of each step is undone by the next one, while the along-the-valley part always points the same way. So add the steps up instead of taking them one at a time. The alternating parts cancel in the sum and the persistent part accumulates.

Run \(\beta\) up from zero on the narrow valley. At \(0\) the grey and coloured paths are the same. By \(0.3\) the crossing collapses after the first step and the path runs down the middle; after fourteen steps it is ten times closer to the bottom than plain descent. Keep going and around \(0.85\) it sails past the centre and loops back — too much memory, and the ball rolls up the far wall.

The rolling-ball picture is worth one sentence and no more: \(v\) is a velocity, the gradient is a force, and \(\beta\) is what is left after friction. It is a good picture and a bad explanation, so do not lean on it.

Say where it goes: this is the standard addition to gradient descent in lecture 4, under this name and with \(\beta\) usually around \(0.9\). It costs one extra vector of memory and no extra gradients.

A step length per coordinate

One \(\alpha\) cannot serve a stiff direction and a slack one.

$$s_i\leftarrow s_i+g_i^2,\qquad x_i\leftarrow x_i-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{s_i}}\,g_i$$

\(g=\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\). Large past gradients, small steps now.

  • The curvature is unknown; the gradients already seen are not
  • Adagrad, RMSProp and Adam vary what goes into \(s\)

Speaker notes

The diagnosis first. The ceiling on \(\alpha\) is set by the stiffest direction, and every other direction then gets a step far below what it could take. The obvious repair is a separate step length per coordinate — small where the function is steep, large where it is flat.

The right numbers would come from the Hessian, and the Hessian is exactly what we are trying not to compute. So use what is already in hand: the gradients seen so far. A coordinate whose gradient has been consistently large gets its step divided by a large number, and a coordinate that has barely moved keeps a big one.

Compare the two paths on the figure and point at the sign. The grey path flips across the valley on every step; the coloured one never crosses it at all, because the stiff coordinate's step has been scaled down to fit. Same gradients, different step lengths, and the zig-zag is simply gone.

Two honest caveats. The running sum \(s\) only grows, so the steps only shrink, and a long run eventually crawls — RMSProp replaces the sum with a decaying average precisely to fix that. And this is a diagonal repair: it scales each coordinate separately and cannot straighten a valley running diagonally, where the Hessian's off-diagonal entries are what matters.

Cut candidate: momentum matters more for lecture 4. If short, state the idea in a sentence and skip the figure.

Newton's method in several variables

Fit a bowl, go to its bottom.

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\nabla f\,h+\tfrac12h^{\mathsf T}Hh$$
$$H\,h=-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}$$
  • Exact in one step on a quadratic, at any stretch
  • An \(n\times n\) solve per step, and \(H\) has \(n^2\) entries

Speaker notes

Same move as in one dimension, with the vector shapes from lecture 2. The gradient is a row, so \(\nabla f\,h\) is a number; the Hessian is square, so \(h^{\mathsf T}Hh\) is a number too. Differentiate the model in \(h\) and set it to zero, and the minimizing step solves \(Hh=-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\).

Note what is not written: \(h=-H^{-1}\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\). Lecture 1 said inverting is the slow and inaccurate way to solve a system, and that applies here. Factor and solve.

Now turn the stretch up and watch the two paths. The grey one gets worse and worse; the single arrow does not change at all, because on a quadratic the model is not an approximation — it is the function, and its bottom is the answer. The condition number simply does not appear in Newton's rate.

Then the bill, because it is what rules the method out later. \(H\) has \(n^2\) entries and the solve costs about \(n^3\). At a million parameters that is a million million entries, and lecture 4 has more parameters than that. This is the reason the methods that actually train networks are the cheap ones from the last three slides.

If someone asks about the middle ground: quasi-Newton methods build an approximation to \(H\) out of the gradients they have already seen. BFGS and L-BFGS are the names; not covered here.

Linear least squares

Eight measurements, two numbers to choose.

$$\min_x\;\lVert Ax-y\rVert^2,\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}1&t_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&t_m\end{bmatrix},\quad x=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$$

Row \(i\) asks for \(a+bt_i=y_i\).

  • More equations than unknowns: no \(x\) satisfies them all
  • Squares, so either sign counts and large errors count more

Speaker notes

The most useful optimization problem there is, and the one case in the hour where the answer comes out of a single linear solve rather than a walk downhill.

Set it up on the figure. Eight points, and a line with two knobs. Each stick is a residual \(r_i=a+bt_i-y_i\), and the number in the readout is the sum of their squares. Let the room call out which way to turn the knobs, and stop when they get it down near \(1.08\) — that is the best there is, and the next slide gets there without any turning.

Why squares, asked every time. Absolute values would do and are sometimes better, but they have a corner at zero and no derivative there, and the minimum comes from no formula. Squares are smooth, the derivative is linear in the parameters, and the minimization collapses. That is the honest reason: squares are the choice that makes the algebra work.

Say that "linear" describes the parameters, not the picture. Fitting a parabola \(a+bt+ct^2\) is still linear least squares, because the unknowns still enter linearly — only the columns of \(A\) change. Section IV goes nonlinear only when the parameters themselves sit inside a nonlinear function.

The normal equations

Set the gradient to zero and solve what is left.

$$\nabla f=2\,(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!A=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;A^{\mathsf T}\!Ax=A^{\mathsf T}y$$
$$\begin{bmatrix}8&28\\28&140\end{bmatrix}x=\begin{pmatrix}22\\98\end{pmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\;x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2pt]\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}$$
  • \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\) is square and symmetric, whatever \(m\) is

The whole minimization, done by one linear solve.

Speaker notes

The derivative of \(\lVert Ax-y\rVert^2\) is the one piece of matrix calculus in the lecture. Write it as \((Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}(Ax-y)\); a step \(h\) changes it by \(2(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!Ah\) plus a term in \(h^2\), so the gradient is the row \(2(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!A\). Setting it to zero and transposing gives \(A^{\mathsf T}\!Ax=A^{\mathsf T}y\).

Do the arithmetic aloud, because it is all small integers. \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\) holds \(m=8\), \(\sum t_i=28\) and \(\sum t_i^2=140\); \(A^{\mathsf T}y\) holds \(\sum y_i=22\) and \(\sum t_iy_i=98\). Solving gives \(b=\tfrac12\) and \(a=1\) exactly, and the sum of squares at that point is \(1.08\) — the number the room was steering towards by hand.

The figure says why one solve is enough: the objective is quadratic in the parameters, so its landscape is a bowl, with one stationary point and that point a minimum. None of the pathologies of section V apply here.

One warning about this matrix. \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\) here has eigenvalues \(145.7\) and \(2.31\), a condition number of about \(63\), and forming it squares whatever conditioning \(A\) had. Software solves least squares by a QR or SVD factorization of \(A\) instead, and never builds \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\). Subtracting the mean of \(t\) first brings the number down to \(5.25\) on its own.

Least squares as orthogonal projection

A perpendicular drop, onto a plane this time.

  • \(Ax\) sweeps out the plane spanned by the columns of \(A\)
  • \(y\) is off that plane
$$A^{\mathsf T}(y-Ax)=0$$

Orthogonal to every column — the normal equations, rearranged.

The best fit is the projection of the data onto what the model can reach.

Speaker notes

Lecture 1 ended its projection slide by saying the idea would come back as least squares. This is that slide, paid off.

Three measurements this time, so the picture fits in three dimensions. The data is the vector \(y=(2,\,1,\,3)\). The model can produce \(Ax=a\,c_1+b\,c_2\), any combination of the two columns \(c_1=(1,1,1)\) and \(c_2=(0,1,2)\), and those sweep out a plane through the origin. The plane is every fit the model is capable of; \(y\) is off it, which is another way of saying the data is not exactly a straight line.

So the question becomes geometric: which point of the plane is nearest \(y\)? The perpendicular drop, exactly as in lecture 1 but onto a plane instead of a line. Here it is \(Ax=(1.5,\,2,\,2.5)\) with \(x=(1.5,\,0.5)\), and the residual \((0.5,\,-1,\,0.5)\) sticks straight out. Check it against both columns in the room: \(0.5-1+0.5=0\), and \(0-1+1=0\).

Then the punchline. "Orthogonal to both columns" written as a matrix is \(A^{\mathsf T}(y-Ax)=0\), which is the normal equations rearranged. The algebra and the geometry are one statement, and neither was derived from the other here — they were derived separately and met.

Rotate it until the residual is seen end-on and the right angle is obvious.

Nonlinear least squares

Same squared error, a model that bends.

$$f(x)=\tfrac12\lVert r(x)\rVert^2,\qquad r_i(x)=m(t_i;x)-y_i$$

Here \(m(t;a,b)=a\,e^{-bt}\), so \(x=(a,\,b)\).

  • \(r\) takes a vector in and gives a vector out: it has a Jacobian \(J\)
  • \(\nabla f=r^{\mathsf T}J\), and no linear solve finds its zero

Speaker notes

The change is small and consequential. \(a\) still enters linearly, but \(b\) sits in the exponent, so the residual is not a linear function of the parameters and \(A\) does not exist. Everything downstream of \(A\) goes with it.

The factor of a half is bookkeeping: it cancels the 2 from differentiating a square, and it is conventional for exactly that reason.

Read the shapes out, since they are all lecture 2's. Six residuals from two parameters, so \(r:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^6\) and its Jacobian is \(6\times2\). The objective is one number, so \(\nabla f\) is a row of two: differentiating \(\tfrac12r^{\mathsf T}r\) gives \(r^{\mathsf T}J\), a \(1\times6\) times a \(6\times2\).

Play with the knobs and let the room feel the difference from the line fit. Turning \(b\) does not slide the curve, it reshapes it, and the two knobs interact — raising \(a\) can be partly undone by raising \(b\). That interaction is the bent valley the next figure shows.

Best they will do by hand is around \(0.015\). Do not dwell; the point is that setting \(r^{\mathsf T}J=0\) is now a pair of nonlinear equations with no formula for their solution, so we are back to walking downhill.

Gauss–Newton

Linearize the residual, not the objective.

$$r(x+h)\approx r(x)+Jh \;\Longrightarrow\;J^{\mathsf T}\!Jh=-J^{\mathsf T}r$$

The normal equations, with \(J\) for \(A\) and \(-r\) for \(y\).

  • \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) stands in for the Hessian, from first derivatives only
  • Close to Newton when the residuals are small

Speaker notes

The trick is to approximate one level down. Rather than fitting a quadratic to \(f\), fit a linear map to \(r\) — which is what a Jacobian is — and then minimize the squared length of that. Substituting \(r+Jh\) into \(\tfrac12\lVert\cdot\rVert^2\) gives a linear least-squares problem in \(h\), and its normal equations are \(J^{\mathsf T}\!Jh=-J^{\mathsf T}r\). Every step of Gauss–Newton is the line-fitting slide again, on a new \(J\).

The payoff is that second derivatives never appear. The true Hessian of \(\tfrac12\lVert r\rVert^2\) is \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) plus a term containing the second derivatives of \(r\), each weighted by its own residual. Drop that term and \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) is left. The approximation is good when the residuals are small — a good fit — or when the model is nearly linear, and it is poor when the fit is bad, which is the honest caveat.

Step the slider from \((1,\,0.2)\) and read the objective out: \(2.11\), \(0.191\), \(0.041\), \(0.0135\), \(0.01343\). Four steps, essentially converged, and the fitted model is \(2.84\,e^{-0.556t}\).

Point at the level curves. They are bent, not elliptical, which is what the previous slide's interacting knobs look like from above. Each Gauss–Newton step pretends they are ellipses and jumps to the centre of the ones it imagines.

Levenberg–Marquardt

Believe the model only so far.

$$\bigl(J^{\mathsf T}\!J+\lambda I\bigr)h=-J^{\mathsf T}r$$
  • \(\lambda\to0\): the Gauss–Newton step
  • \(\lambda\) large: a short step along \(-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\)
  • Raise \(\lambda\) when a step makes \(f\) worse, lower it when it helps

\(\lambda\) is a trust region in disguise: it sets how far out the model is believed.

Speaker notes

Start with the failure, at \((1,\,0.95)\) where \(f=2.96\). The undamped Gauss–Newton step is long, leaves the frame, and drives \(b\) negative — at which point \(e^{-bt}\) grows instead of decaying and the objective reaches \(10^4\). The step was not wrong; the linear model was simply trusted far outside the region where it holds.

Now push \(\lambda\) up and watch the arrow. It shortens and swings round, and by \(\lambda=1\) it lands at \(f=0.35\) — an excellent step. Push further and it lines up with the negative gradient, at \(19^\circ\), and becomes so short that it achieves almost nothing. The two ends of the slider are Gauss–Newton and steepest descent, and the useful steps are in the middle.

Why adding \(\lambda I\) does that: as \(\lambda\) grows it swamps \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\), the solve tends to \(h\approx-J^{\mathsf T}r/\lambda\), and \(J^{\mathsf T}r\) is the gradient transposed. It also makes the matrix invertible when \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) is not, which is a second reason it is there.

The trust-region reading, since the claim asserts it. Restricting the step to \(\lVert h\rVert\le\Delta\) and minimizing the model inside that ball gives exactly this equation, with \(\lambda\) the multiplier of the constraint: every \(\lambda\) corresponds to a radius, and the adaptive rule — grow \(\Delta\) when the model predicted the drop well, shrink it when it did not — is the same rule as the third bullet, upside down.

This is the standard curve-fitting algorithm, and what most software means by "fit".

Stationary points

Everything here stops where the gradient vanishes.

$$\nabla f=0$$

A minimum, a maximum, a saddle, or a flat stretch.

  • \(\tfrac14x^4-\tfrac13x^3-x^2+1\) has three
  • Necessary for a minimum, and nothing more

Which one an algorithm stops at is decided by where it started.

Speaker notes

A new curve, because the old one only had one dip. Its derivative is \(x^3-x^2-2x=x(x-2)(x+1)\), which factors by inspection and vanishes at \(-1\), \(0\) and \(2\). Drive the marker through all three and let the lower panel cross zero each time.

They are three different things. At \(-1\) there is a dip of depth \(0.583\); at \(2\) a deeper one at \(-1.667\); at \(0\) a peak of \(1\). Every method in this lecture halts at all three equally happily, because all three satisfy the only condition any of them tests.

Two words worth having. The dip at \(2\) is the global minimum: no point anywhere is lower. The dip at \(-1\) is a local minimum: no point nearby is lower, and that is all. Nothing in the hour finds the first except by luck.

The fourth case has no picture here because this curve has none: a flat stretch, where the derivative is zero over an interval or touches zero without crossing, as \(x^3\) does at the origin. The classification on the next slide says nothing at all about those.

The second-derivative test

The bends say which kind of point it is.

$$\text{eigenvalues of }H:\quad \text{all}>0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{minimum}$$

All negative, a maximum. Mixed signs, a saddle. A zero, and the test is silent.

  • \(x^2-y^2\) bends \(+2\) along one axis and \(-2\) along the other
  • In one dimension \(H\) is one number, and this is its sign

Speaker notes

The Hessian was introduced last week as the bend of the landscape in every direction at once, and its eigenvalues as the bends along its own axes. This is what they are for.

Rotate the saddle and take the two cuts one at a time. Along \(x\) the surface is \(x^2\), a valley, and the centre is its lowest point. Along \(y\) it is \(-y^2\), a ridge, and the same centre is its highest point. Both partial derivatives are zero there, so the gradient vanishes, and the point is neither a minimum nor a maximum. \(H=\operatorname{diag}(2,\,-2)\), and the mixed signs are the whole diagnosis.

Say plainly what the test costs. It needs \(H\), and section III already explained why nobody computes \(H\) when there are a million parameters. In practice it is a piece of understanding rather than a step that gets run.

A remark for lecture 4, worth half a minute. In high dimensions a stationary point is a minimum only if every one of the millions of eigenvalues is positive, and that is a lot of coin flips to win at once. Saddle points hugely outnumber local minima there. The good news is that a saddle has a downhill direction and gradient descent, especially with noise, tends to slide off one.

Cut candidate: the figure carries the idea; the high-dimensional remark can go.

Local minima

Where you start decides where you stop.

  • A descent method only ever goes down
  • The basin it starts in is the basin it ends in
  • Here the divide sits at the peak, \(x=0\)

Nothing local can see over the hill.

Speaker notes

Sweep the start from left to right and let the room watch the answer jump. From anywhere left of the peak the path runs into the shallow dip at \(-1\); from anywhere right of it, into the deep one at \(2\). Nothing gradual happens in between — the answer flips as the start crosses \(x=0\).

Put the start almost exactly on the peak and let it sit for a moment: the gradient is nearly zero, the steps are tiny, and it takes a long time to make up its mind. Flat regions are slow for exactly this reason.

There is no repair inside this framework, and it is worth being blunt about it. The method only knows \(f\) and \(\nabla f\) where it stands; the deeper dip is not visible from the shallow one at any distance. Finding the global minimum of a general function needs a fundamentally different kind of search.

End on the practical note, because it is the one that matters for lecture 4: for a big network nobody finds the global minimum and nobody tries. Training stops at a point that is good enough, and the surprise of the last decade is that on these landscapes such points are usually fine.

Global methods

Ways to look in more than one basin.

  • Many random starts; keep the best
  • Accept an uphill step now and then, less often as time passes
  • Keep a population of guesses; combine and disturb them
$$\text{no guarantee, and no free lunch}$$

Speaker notes

One slide, deliberately, on a subject with whole books. The point is to mark the edge of what has been covered, not to cross it.

The first line is the only one anyone should reach for first: run a local method from many starting points and keep the best answer. It is trivial to implement, it parallelizes perfectly, and on the figure nine of twelve starts land in the deeper dip. The basins here happen to be roughly the size they look; in high dimensions they need not be, and random starts can miss a narrow one every time.

The second is simulated annealing: allow steps that make \(f\) worse, with a probability that falls as the run goes on, so the search wanders early and settles late. The third covers genetic and evolutionary algorithms and particle swarms.

Say the caveat plainly. None of these guarantees the global minimum on a general function — no method can, without assumptions, since the minimum may sit in a dip too narrow ever to be sampled. What they buy is a better chance for the evaluations spent.

Cut candidate: this slide and the constraints slide are the two to drop if the hour is short. Say the first bullet and move on.

Constrained optimization

The answer need not have zero gradient.

$$\min_x f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x)=0$$
$$\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g$$
  • The contour of \(f\) touches the constraint rather than crossing it
  • \(x^2+2y^2\) on \(x+y=\tfrac32\): the answer is \((1,\,\tfrac12)\)

Speaker notes

Until now \(x\) could be anything. Most real problems come with conditions: a budget, a fixed total, a quantity that cannot go negative.

The argument is geometric and takes one minute. Stand on the line. If the contour of \(f\) through that point crosses the line, then moving a little one way along the line goes to a lower contour, so the point is not the answer. Only where the contour is tangent to the line does no direction along the line go down. Tangency means the two normals are parallel, and the normals are the gradients: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\). The number \(\lambda\) is a Lagrange multiplier.

Check it on the figure with real numbers. At \((1,\,\tfrac12)\) the gradient of \(x^2+2y^2\) is \((2,\,2)\) and the gradient of \(x+y\) is \((1,\,1)\), so \(\lambda=2\). The unconstrained minimum is the origin, which is not on the line, and the constrained value is \(1.5\).

Inequalities in a sentence: for \(g(x)\le0\) either the answer is inside, where the constraint may be ignored, or on the boundary, where the equality case applies with a sign condition on \(\lambda\). Those are the KKT conditions. Named, not developed.

Stochastic gradient descent

Estimate the gradient from part of the sum.

$$\nabla f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f_i \;\approx\;\frac{m}{\lvert B\rvert}\sum_{i\in B}\nabla f_i$$

\(B\) is a handful of terms, drawn afresh each step.

  • Each step costs \(\lvert B\rvert\) terms instead of \(m\)
  • The noise never dies: the path settles into a cloud

A worse gradient, taken often enough, gets there first.

Speaker notes

The last slide of the hour, and the one lecture 4 starts from. Every objective fitted today was a sum over data points, and so is the loss of a network. A full gradient touches every term; with a million terms that is a million evaluations for one step.

But the sum has structure worth exploiting: each term contributes a vector, and a handful of terms, scaled up, estimates the total. The estimate is unbiased and noisy, and it costs a fraction as much.

The slider fixes the budget rather than the step count — sixty-four single terms every time — which is the comparison that matters, because terms are what gets paid for. At batch 8 the path is smooth and beautiful and only gets eight steps, ending far from the answer at \(f=5.4\). At batch 1 it is a drunkard's walk that takes sixty-four steps and ends at \(0.58\), against a best possible \(0.54\).

Point at the cloud at the end of the batch-1 path. The noise does not shrink as the answer is approached, so the path never settles — it orbits. Shrinking \(\alpha\) over the run shrinks the cloud, which is what a learning-rate schedule is for.

One last remark, then stop: the noise is not only a cost. A noisy step can leave a shallow dip that a clean gradient would have stayed in. On the landscapes of lecture 4 that is thought to be part of why it works.

Quiz

Four questions, hands up.

Speaker notes

Multiple choice, answered from the room. Read each one out, count hands for each letter, then give the answer. If time is short, ask 1 and 2: the descent condition and the zig-zag are what lecture 4 leans on hardest.

At a point where \(\nabla f=(2,\;6)\), which direction goes downhill?

  1. A\((1,\;1)\)
  2. B\((3,\;-1)\)
  3. C\((-1,\;1)\)
  4. D\((1,\;-1)\)

Speaker notes

Answer: D. The test is \(\nabla f\,d<0\), one multiplication and one addition per option. A gives \(2+6=8\), C gives \(-2+6=4\); both go uphill. D gives \(2-6=-4\), the only negative one.

B is the trap and worth the time: \(6-6=0\), so \(f\) does not change at all to first order. That direction runs along the level curve through the point, at right angles to the gradient. Anyone who picked it was thinking "perpendicular to the gradient must be a good idea" — it is the one direction that achieves nothing.

Follow-up if the room is quick: which is the steepest descent direction? None of them; it is \(-(2,6)=(-2,-6)\).

Steepest descent on a quadratic whose Hessian has eigenvalues \(1\) and \(100\) will

  1. AReach the minimum in one step
  2. BZig-zag across a narrow valley
  3. CDiverge, whatever the step length
  4. DFollow the same path as Newton's method

Speaker notes

Answer: B. The condition number is \(100\), so the bowl is a hundred times stiffer one way than the other, and even the best fixed step leaves \((\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)=99/101\approx0.980\) of the error behind each time. That is about \(230\) steps for a hundredfold reduction.

A is what happens when \(\kappa=1\) and the contours are circles. C is wrong but tempting: it diverges only for \(\alpha\ge2/\lambda_{\max}=0.02\), and below that it converges — slowly. D is exactly backwards: Newton's method is the one that ignores the conditioning, because on a quadratic its model is the function itself and one step lands at the bottom.

If someone asks how big \(\kappa\) gets in practice: the eight-point line fit earlier in the hour was already \(63\).

At the least-squares solution, the residual \(y-Ax\) is

  1. AZero
  2. BOrthogonal to every column of \(A\)
  3. COrthogonal to \(y\)
  4. DParallel to the columns of \(A\)

Speaker notes

Answer: B. That is \(A^{\mathsf T}(y-Ax)=0\) read out in words, and it is the normal equations rearranged. Geometrically: \(Ax\) is the projection of \(y\) onto the plane the columns span, and what is left over sticks out of that plane.

A is the trap for anyone still thinking of \(Ax=y\) as a system to be solved exactly. With more equations than unknowns there is usually no such \(x\), and the residual is zero only in the lucky case where the data already lies on the model. D is the opposite of the answer: anything parallel to the columns is reachable by the model, so it would be folded into \(Ax\) rather than left over. C confuses the data with the model's reach; the residual is orthogonal to the plane, not to \(y\), and in the three-point example \((0.5,-1,0.5)\cdot(2,1,3)=1.5\neq0\).

Newton's method converges to a point where \(\nabla f=0\). That point is

  1. AAlways the global minimum
  2. BAlways a local minimum
  3. CA minimum, a maximum or a saddle
  4. DA local minimum, unless \(f\) is quadratic

Speaker notes

Answer: C. The method solves \(\nabla f=0\), and that equation is satisfied by all three kinds of point. The figure earlier showed it: started to the left of the origin on the cubic, it converged neatly onto the peak at \(x=-1\).

A ignores everything the landscape section said — the method is local and cannot see another basin. B and D both assume the method checks which kind of point it found, and it does not; nothing in \(Hh=-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\) tests the sign of anything. D has it backwards as well: a quadratic is the one case that is safe, because a bowl has exactly one stationary point.

Follow-up: what would make it safe in general? Check that \(H\) is positive definite and, when it is not, fall back to a damped step — which is Levenberg–Marquardt's \(\lambda\), doing that job.
