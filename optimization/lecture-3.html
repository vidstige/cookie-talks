The minimization problem

Find the input that makes the output smallest.

$$x^\star=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\;f(x)$$

The place, not the value.

  • Maximizing \(f\) is minimizing \(-f\)
  • Here \(f(x)=\tfrac14x^3-\tfrac34x+1\), on \([-0.4,\,2.6]\)

An optimizer never sees the function — only the values it asks for.

Speaker notes

The same curve as last week, given a job. Over this stretch it falls and then rises, and the bottom sits at \(x=1\) with \(f(1)=\tfrac14-\tfrac34+1=0.5\).

Separate the two things the figure marks, because they are confused constantly. The minimum is \(0.5\), a value on the vertical axis. The minimizer is \(1\), a place on the horizontal axis. Optimization wants the place. In lecture 4 the value will be a loss nobody cares about and the place will be the weights of a network, which is the whole product.

The claim is the rule for the next few slides, and it is worth dwelling on. The algorithm is handed a black box: it may name an \(x\) and be told \(f(x)\), and nothing else. It cannot see the shape on the screen. Every method in the hour is a strategy for choosing which \(x\) to ask about next.

Maximizing is the same problem upside down, so nothing is lost by only ever minimizing. Say it once and never mention maxima again until the landscape.

Load-bearing: the distinction between the minimum and the minimizer is used on every slide after this one.

Local minima

Where you start decides where you stop.

  • Step downhill from a start: it ends in the dip below it
  • Global: lowest anywhere. Local: lowest nearby
  • Here the divide sits at the peak, \(x=0\)

Speaker notes

Before any method, the trap every method in the hour shares. A new curve, \(\tfrac14x^4-\tfrac13x^3-x^2+1\), with two dips of different depth: \(0.583\) at \(-1\) and \(-1.667\) at \(2\), and a peak at \(0\) between them.

Two words worth having. The dip at \(2\) is the global minimum: no point anywhere is lower. The dip at \(-1\) is a local minimum: no point nearby is lower, and that is all.

The path on the figure is the simplest method there is: from a start, keep stepping downhill. Sweep the start from left to right and let the room watch the answer jump. From anywhere left of the peak the path runs into the shallow dip; from anywhere right of it, into the deep one. Nothing gradual happens in between — the answer flips as the start crosses \(x=0\).

Put the start almost exactly on the peak and let it sit for a moment: the slope is nearly zero, the steps are tiny, and it takes a long time to make up its mind. Flat regions are slow for exactly this reason.

Be blunt about it. A method that only knows \(f\) and its slope where it stands cannot see the deeper dip from the shallow one at any distance. Every method in this lecture is local in that sense, so keep this figure in mind for the rest of the hour.

The practical note, because it matters for lecture 4: for a big network nobody finds the global minimum and nobody tries. Training stops at a point that is good enough, and the surprise of the last decade is that on these landscapes such points are usually fine.

Bracketing a minimum

Three points that trap it.

$$a<b<c,\qquad f(b)<f(a),\quad f(b)<f(c)$$

Then \(f\) has a minimum somewhere in \((a,\,c)\).

  • Two comparisons, no arithmetic
  • Going down and then up again has to turn somewhere

Speaker notes

Slide \(b\) and read the shading out loud. Over most of the range \(f(b)\) is below both ends and the shading is there: a minimum is trapped, and no further evaluation can lose it. Push \(b\) out past about \(1.9\) and the shading goes, because \(f(b)\) has climbed back above \(f(a)\). Three points on a curve that simply rises look exactly like that, and nothing is trapped.

Why it works, in one sentence: the curve gets from \(f(a)\) down to \(f(b)\) and back up to \(f(c)\), so it turns around inside. That is the whole argument, and it needs continuity and nothing else — no derivative, no formula, not even arithmetic beyond comparing two numbers.

Name the assumption honestly while the slider is in hand: this finds a minimum in the bracket, not the lowest point of the function. If the curve wiggled several times between \(a\) and \(c\) the bracket would still be valid and would still trap only one of the dips. That is the local minimum of the last slide again.

The next slide keeps the bracket property true and makes the bracket narrower. That is the entire design.

Halving the bracket

Two probes, one either side of the middle.

  • Compare \(f\) at \(m-\varepsilon\) and \(m+\varepsilon\)
  • Keep the side holding the smaller one
  • Two evaluations bought half the width
$$\sqrt{0.5}\approx0.707\ \text{per evaluation}$$

Speaker notes

Walk the slider out one step at a time and say what each step does. Two fresh points are placed a hair apart around the middle of the bracket. Whichever is lower, the minimum is on that side, because the curve is already rising by the time it reaches the other probe. Throw the far half away and repeat. Textbooks call this dichotomous search.

The readout carries the argument: the width goes 3, 1.62, 0.875, 0.47, while the evaluation count goes 2, 4, 6, 8. It is a little over half each time rather than exactly half, and the excess is the gap between the probes, which shrinks along with the bracket. Say the comparison out loud in the form that matters — not per step, but per evaluation, because evaluations are what a real objective charges for. Half the width for two evaluations is \(\sqrt{0.5}\), about \(0.707\), each time we ask.

Then name the limit. Every step cuts the bracket by the same factor, so each correct digit costs the same number of evaluations, about seven, and shrinking the bracket a thousandfold takes twenty. Placing the probes more cleverly trims that constant but leaves the rate alone. The next section changes the rate, by asking for the slope.

If asked about \(\varepsilon\): it has to be small enough to be inside the bracket and large enough that the two values differ in floating point. The figure exaggerates it so the two probes can be told apart.

Cut candidate: if the hour is running long, state the halving rate in a sentence and move on to the derivative.

Where the derivative vanishes

The first thing a slope says about a lowest point.

$$\frac{df}{dx}(x^\star)=0$$

True at every minimum. Also true elsewhere.

$$\tfrac34\bigl(x^2-1\bigr)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;x=-1,\ x=1$$

Zero slope is a clue, not a proof.

Speaker notes

The argument takes one sentence and is worth saying slowly. If the slope at a point is not zero, then one of the two directions goes downhill, so a small step that way lowers \(f\), so the point was not the lowest. Only a zero slope survives.

Drive the marker across and read the lower panel: left of \(-1\) the slope is positive, between \(-1\) and \(1\) it is negative, right of \(1\) positive again. It crosses zero twice. The derivative is \(\tfrac34(x^2-1)\), which vanishes at \(x=\pm1\), and that is arithmetic anyone can do.

Now the claim, which is why the lecture does not end here. At \(x=1\) the curve has its lowest point. At \(x=-1\) it has a peak, and the slope is every bit as zero. Solving \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\) does not distinguish them. Say that the fix is the second derivative and leave it there for now; it comes back with a name attached.

Two honest limits. Solving \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\) by hand only works when the derivative is something we can solve, which it usually is not. And with a vector going in it becomes a system of equations, not one equation. Both are reasons to search rather than solve.

Load-bearing: Newton's method is built directly on this slide, and so are the normal equations, which are the first such system.

Stationary points

Every place where the slope is zero.

$$\frac{df}{dx}=0$$

A minimum, a maximum, or neither.

  • \(3x^5-5x^3\) has three: a peak, a pause, a dip
  • Necessary for a minimum, and nothing more

Which one an algorithm stops at is decided by where it started.

Speaker notes

A new curve, built to have one of each kind. Its slope is \(15x^4-15x^2=15x^2(x-1)(x+1)\), which vanishes at \(-1\), \(0\) and \(1\). Drive the marker through all three and watch the lower panel.

At \(-1\) the slope goes from positive to negative: a peak, of height \(2\). At \(1\) it goes from negative to positive: a dip, at \(-2\). At \(0\) it touches zero and turns back without changing sign. The curve was falling, pauses for an instant, and keeps falling. That is neither a minimum nor a maximum.

Every method in this lecture halts at all three equally happily, because all three satisfy the only condition any of them tests. The pause is the sneaky one. A descent method arriving from the left slows to a crawl there, because the slope near zero is tiny, and it creeps towards the pause without ever getting past.

The second derivative tells them apart. Here it is \(60x^3-30x\): \(-30\) at the peak, \(30\) at the dip, and \(0\) at the pause, where the test says nothing.

Newton's method for equations

Follow the tangent down to zero, and start again from there.

$$x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{g(x_n)}{g'(x_n)}$$

Where the tangent line at \(x_n\) crosses zero. The prime is the slope: \(g'=\frac{dg}{dx}\).

  • \(g(x)=x^2-2\), from \(1\): \(1.5\), \(1.4167\), \(1.414216\)
  • The correct digits double each step

Replace the curve by its tangent, and solve that instead.

Speaker notes

A short detour into solving equations, because the fastest minimization method of the hour is an equation solver in disguise. The problem: find where \(g(x)=0\), starting from a guess.

The idea is lecture 2's tangent line. Near \(x_n\), \(g(x_n+h)\approx g(x_n)+g'(x_n)\,h\). A line is easy to solve: it is zero at \(h=-g(x_n)/g'(x_n)\), which is the step on the slide. The line is not the curve, so that answer is not exact, but it is much closer, and from there the same step is taken again.

Say once why the prime appears here and nowhere else in the hour: the step is a ratio of a value and a slope at the same point, and the prime keeps that ratio readable. It means exactly \(\frac{dg}{dx}\), as named in lecture 2.

Step the slider from \(1\) on \(x^2-2\), whose root is \(\sqrt2\), and read the errors: \(0.41\), \(0.086\), \(0.0025\), \(0.0000021\). Each is roughly the square of the one before, so the number of correct digits doubles every step. Four steps give \(\sqrt2\) to eleven digits.

Then show how it fails. Drag the start towards zero, where the tangent is nearly flat: the first step is thrown far out, and here it happens to recover. Exactly at zero the step divides by zero. Newton's method is fast near an answer and promises nothing far from one.

A footnote for anyone who wants it: here the step simplifies to \(x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+2/x_n)\), the Babylonian method for square roots, some four thousand years older than Newton.

Newton's method for a minimum

Solve \(f'(x)=0\) the same way.

$$x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f'(x_n)}{f''(x_n)}$$

Newton's method with \(g=f'\): the second derivative \(f''\) is the slope of the slope.

  • From \(2\): \(1.25\), \(1.025\), \(1.0003\), \(1.00000005\)
  • Two derivatives per step, and very few steps

It finds where the slope is zero. It does not ask which way the curve bends.

Speaker notes

Now point the method of the last slide at the equation from the derivative slide. A minimum has \(f'(x)=0\), so take \(g=f'\). Its slope is the second derivative \(f''\), and the step becomes the one on the slide. Nothing else is new.

The figure has two curves: \(f\) in red and its slope in blue. The dashed tangent is drawn on the slope, not on \(f\), and where it crosses zero is the next guess. Step it from \(2\): the errors are \(1\), \(0.25\), \(0.025\), \(0.0003\), \(0.00000005\), each roughly the square of the one before, as with the square root.

Then drag the start to the left of zero and let the room watch it converge happily to \(x=-1\), which is the peak. The slope is zero there too, and the method only ever solves \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\): the sign of the bend never enters. At exactly \(x=0\) the bend is zero and the step is undefined.

The same step has a second reading, for anyone who asks. It jumps to the bottom of the parabola that matches \(f\) in value, slope and bend at \(x\). Same formula, another picture.

The practical fix, named and not developed: when the bend is negative or the step is wild, take a shorter step, or fall back to a plain downhill one.

Load-bearing: Gauss–Newton is this slide in several variables, with \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) for the bend.

Linear least squares

Eight measurements, two numbers to choose.

$$\min_x\;\lVert Ax-y\rVert^2$$

More equations than unknowns, so no \(x\) solves \(Ax=y\) exactly.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&t_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&t_8\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$$
  • Row \(i\) asks for \(a+bt_i=y_i\)
  • Squares, so either sign counts and large errors count more

Speaker notes

The most useful optimization problem there is, and the one case in the hour where the answer comes out of a single linear solve rather than a walk downhill.

Set it up on the figure. Eight points, and a line with two knobs. Each stick is a residual \(r_i=a+bt_i-y_i\), and the number in the readout is the sum of their squares. Let the room call out which way to turn the knobs, and stop when they get it down near \(1.08\) — that is the best there is, and the next slide gets there without any turning.

Why squares, asked every time. Absolute values would do and are sometimes better, but they have a corner at zero and no derivative there, and the minimum comes from no formula. Squares are smooth, the derivative is linear in the parameters, and the minimization collapses. That is the honest reason: squares are the choice that makes the algebra work.

Say that "linear" describes the parameters, not the picture. Fitting a parabola \(a+bt+ct^2\) is still linear least squares, because the unknowns still enter linearly — only the columns of \(A\) change. Two slides on, the fit goes nonlinear, which happens only when the parameters themselves sit inside a nonlinear function.

The normal equations

Set the gradient to zero and solve what is left.

$$f(x)=\lVert Ax-y\rVert^2,\qquad \nabla f=0$$

At the lowest point the slope is zero along every coordinate at once.

$$\nabla f=2\,(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!A=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;A^{\mathsf T}\!Ax=A^{\mathsf T}y$$
  • Rearranged: \(A^{\mathsf T}(y-Ax)=0\), so the residual is orthogonal to every column

The whole minimization, done by one linear solve.

Speaker notes

State the objective first, exactly as on the last slide, and say what is wanted: its lowest point. In one dimension that meant \(\frac{df}{dx}=0\). With a vector going in, the slope has to be zero along every coordinate at once, so every partial derivative is zero, and together the partial derivatives are the gradient. Hence \(\nabla f=0\): one equation per unknown, two here.

The derivative of \(\lVert Ax-y\rVert^2\) is the one piece of matrix calculus in the lecture. Write it as \((Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}(Ax-y)\); a step \(h\) changes it by \(2(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!Ah\) plus a term in \(h^2\), so the gradient is the row \(2(Ax-y)^{\mathsf T}\!A\). Setting it to zero and transposing gives \(A^{\mathsf T}\!Ax=A^{\mathsf T}y\).

The figure is the last slide's, with the line now always the solution: every time a point moves, the two equations are solved again. Start from the readout. \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\) holds \(m=8\), \(\sum t_i=28\) and \(\sum t_i^2=140\), and it never changes, because it only knows where the measurements were taken. \(A^{\mathsf T}y\) holds \(\sum y_i=22\) and \(\sum t_iy_i=98\). Solving gives \(a=1\) and \(b=\tfrac12\) exactly, and the sum of squares is \(1.08\), the number the room was steering towards by hand. Then drag the last point up and watch the line tilt towards it.

The bullet is lecture 1's projection, back as promised. The residual is at right angles to every column of \(A\), so the best fit is the orthogonal projection of the data onto what the model can reach. Named, not drawn.

Why one solve is enough: the objective is quadratic in the parameters, so its landscape is a bowl, with one stationary point and that point a minimum. None of the trouble from the local-minima slide applies here.

One warning about this matrix. \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\) here has eigenvalues \(145.7\) and \(2.31\), a condition number of about \(63\), and forming it squares whatever conditioning \(A\) had. Software solves least squares by a QR or SVD factorization of \(A\) instead, and never builds \(A^{\mathsf T}\!A\). Subtracting the mean of \(t\) first brings the number down to \(5.25\) on its own.

Nonlinear least squares

Same squared error, a model that bends.

$$\min_x\;f(x)=\tfrac12\lVert r(x)\rVert^2$$

\(r\) is the vector of residuals, nonlinear in the unknowns \(x\).

$$r_i(a,b)=a\,e^{-bt_i}-y_i,\qquad x=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$$
  • \(t_i\) and \(y_i\): six measurements, fixed numbers
  • Vector in, vector out: \(r\) has a Jacobian \(J\)
  • \(\nabla f=r^{\mathsf T}J\), and no linear solve finds its zero

Speaker notes

The change is small and consequential. \(a\) still enters linearly, but \(b\) sits in the exponent, so the residual is not a linear function of the parameters and \(A\) does not exist. Everything downstream of \(A\) goes with it.

Point at what varies. The \(t_i\) and \(y_i\) are the measurements, six pairs of fixed numbers, so the residuals are functions of \(a\) and \(b\) alone. In code they are constants the residual function reads, not arguments it takes.

The factor of a half is bookkeeping: it cancels the 2 from differentiating a square, and it is conventional for exactly that reason.

Read the shapes out, since they are all lecture 2's. Six residuals from two parameters, so \(r:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^6\) and its Jacobian is \(6\times2\). The objective is one number, so \(\nabla f\) is a row of two: differentiating \(\tfrac12r^{\mathsf T}r\) gives \(r^{\mathsf T}J\), a \(1\times6\) times a \(6\times2\).

Play with the knobs and let the room feel the difference from the line fit. Turning \(b\) does not slide the curve, it reshapes it, and the two knobs interact — raising \(a\) can be partly undone by raising \(b\). That interaction is the bent valley the next figure shows.

Best they will do by hand is around \(0.015\). Do not dwell; the point is that setting \(r^{\mathsf T}J=0\) is now a pair of nonlinear equations with no formula for their solution, so the answer has to be approached step by step.

Gauss–Newton

Newton's method in several variables.

$$x_{n+1}=x_n-\bigl(J^{\mathsf T}J\bigr)^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}\,r(x_n)$$

Newton's \(x_{n+1}=x_n-f'(x_n)/f''(x_n)\), with \(J^{\mathsf T}r\) for \(f'\) and \(J^{\mathsf T}J\) for \(f''\). \(J\) is the Jacobian of \(r\), evaluated at \(x_n\).

x = np.array([1.0, 0.2])                # a, b
for n in range(4):
    a, b = x
    e = np.exp(-b * t)
    r = a * e - y                       # 6 residuals
    J = np.stack([e, -a * t * e], 1)    # 6 × 2
    x = x - np.linalg.solve(J.T @ J, J.T @ r)
  • \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) stands in for the Hessian, from first derivatives only

Speaker notes

The trick is to approximate one level down. Rather than applying Newton's method to \(\nabla f=0\), which needs the second derivatives of \(r\), fit a linear map to \(r\) — which is what a Jacobian is — and then minimize the squared length of that. Substituting \(r+Jh\) into \(\tfrac12\lVert\cdot\rVert^2\) gives a linear least-squares problem in \(h\), and its normal equations are \(J^{\mathsf T}\!Jh=-J^{\mathsf T}r\). Solve for \(h\) and add it, and that is the formula on the slide. Every step of Gauss–Newton is the line-fitting slide again, on a new \(J\).

Point at the pattern from one dimension: the slope divided by the bend, subtracted. Here the slope is the gradient \(J^{\mathsf T}r\) and the bend is \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\), and dividing by a matrix means multiplying by its inverse. The subtitle is honest only up to that bend. True Newton would use the full Hessian of \(f\): with \(d\) unknowns it has \(d^2\) entries, needs second derivatives, and costs about \(d^3\) to solve with. At a million parameters that is out of reach, which is why the next section's methods use the gradient alone. The inverse is notation: the code calls a solver instead, the fast and accurate way lecture 1 recommended.

Then read the code, because it is the whole algorithm. Each pass evaluates the six residuals and the \(6\times2\) Jacobian at the current point: the two columns are the derivatives of \(a\,e^{-bt}\) with respect to \(a\) and to \(b\). It solves one \(2\times2\) system and subtracts the result. Nothing is tuned: there is no step length to choose, unlike the downhill methods of the next section.

The payoff is that second derivatives never appear. The true Hessian of \(\tfrac12\lVert r\rVert^2\) is \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) plus a term containing the second derivatives of \(r\), each weighted by its own residual. Drop that term and \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) is left. The approximation is good when the residuals are small — a good fit — or when the model is nearly linear, and then the step is close to Newton's. It is poor when the fit is bad, which is the honest caveat.

Step the slider from \((1,\,0.2)\) and read the objective out: \(2.11\), \(0.191\), \(0.041\), \(0.0135\), \(0.01343\). Four steps, essentially converged, and the fitted model is \(2.84\,e^{-0.556t}\).

The figure is the first landscape seen from above, and the next section draws every figure this way. Each curve joins the points \((a,\,b)\) where \(f\) has one value: last week's surfaces, flattened onto the page.

Point at the level curves. They are bent, not elliptical, which is what the previous slide's interacting knobs look like from above. Each Gauss–Newton step pretends they are ellipses and jumps to the centre of the ones it imagines.

Descent directions

Which way is downhill, when there are many ways to go.

$$\nabla f\,d<0$$

A row times a column: the rate of change of \(f\) along \(d\), called the directional derivative.

  • Zero along the level curve — neither up nor down
  • Most negative opposite the gradient

A descent method picks a direction, then a distance.

Speaker notes

The rings are the level curves of \(x^2+2y^2\), seen from above like the curve fit on the Gauss–Newton slide.

The gradient at \((1.5,\,0.8)\) is the row \((3,\;3.2)\), and the number in the readout is \(3d_1+3.2d_2\). Sweep the angle all the way round and narrate it: over half the circle the number is negative and \(f\) falls, over the other half it rises, and at exactly two angles it is zero. Those two are the directions along the ring, where a small step changes nothing — the fact the gradient sits at right angles to its level curve, from lecture 2, now visible as a number passing through zero.

Any direction in the falling half will do, and that freedom is what the rest of the lecture spends. The most negative is \(d=-\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\), which is the obvious choice and, as this section shows, often a poor one.

Worth saying once: this is the linear approximation from lecture 2 doing its job. \(f(x+\alpha d)\approx f(x)+\alpha\,\nabla f\,d\), so for small enough \(\alpha\) the sign of \(\nabla f\,d\) decides the sign of the change. The promise is only for small \(\alpha\), and the step-length slide deals with that.

Gradient descent

Take the direction the gradient forbids most strongly.

$$x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha\,\nabla f(x_n)^{\mathsf T}$$

Transposed: the gradient is a row, the point is a column.

  • Needs one gradient per step and nothing else
  • Each step lands where the ring it started on is steepest

Steepest at a point is not quickest to the bottom.

Speaker notes

The obvious method, and the one everything in machine learning is descended from. Stand somewhere, ask the gradient which way is up, walk the other way, repeat.

Step it out and point at the right angles: every step leaves its ring perpendicular, because that is what the gradient does. With a fixed \(\alpha\) the steps also shrink as the path goes in, without anyone arranging it — the gradient itself gets smaller near the bottom. That is a real convenience and worth naming.

The name: gradient descent, and in older books steepest descent, because the step goes the way the function falls fastest. With a fixed \(\alpha\) it is exactly what lecture 4 trains networks with.

The claim is the seed of the rest of this section. Steepest is a statement about an infinitesimal step at one point. It says nothing about where the bottom is, and on a stretched bowl the two directions disagree badly. This figure is mild; the zig-zag slide turns the stretch up.

On the transpose, briefly, since lecture 2 made the choice deliberately: \(\nabla f\) is a row so that \(\nabla f\,h\) is a matrix times a step. To move a point we need a column, so it gets transposed. Anyone writing gradients as columns will not see the transpose in their books; nothing else changes.

Choosing a step length

One number between crawling and blowing up.

  • Too small: many steps, little progress
  • Too large: the value climbs, and keeps climbing
  • On \(x^2+2y^2\), any \(\alpha\ge\tfrac12\) fails to converge
$$y_{n+1}=(1-4\alpha)\,y_n$$

Speaker notes

Sweep \(\alpha\) slowly from the left and narrate three regimes. Small: the path is a tidy curve that has barely started after twelve steps. Around a third: it drops almost straight in. Past a half: the path throws itself across the bowl and each throw is bigger than the last.

The algebra is one line, and the figure is its picture. On this bowl the update is \(x_{n+1}=(1-2\alpha)x_n\) and \(y_{n+1}=(1-4\alpha)y_n\), so each coordinate is multiplied by a fixed number every step. Convergence needs both multipliers inside \((-1,1)\), and the \(y\) one leaves at \(\alpha=\tfrac12\). The threshold is set by the largest bend, which is \(4\): the rule is \(\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\).

Note which coordinate breaks first, because it is the point of the next slide. The steep direction blows up while the shallow one is still crawling. A single \(\alpha\) has to keep the steepest direction stable, and that ceiling is then imposed on every other direction too.

Where this shows up later: in lecture 4 this number is called the learning rate, it is the first thing anyone tunes, and the failure on the right of this slider is exactly what a diverging loss curve looks like.

The zig-zag

A narrow valley gets crossed rather than followed.

$$\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}},\qquad \text{per step}\;\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$$

Eigenvalues of the Hessian: the stiffest bend over the slackest.

  • \(\kappa=1\): one step. \(\kappa=100\): \(0.980\) of the error left
  • Even the best fixed \(\alpha\) zig-zags

The gradient points across the valley, not along it.

Speaker notes

Lecture 2 promised this slide on the Hessian, so pay it off by name. The condition number is the ratio of the largest bend to the smallest, and the eigenvalues of the Hessian are those bends — lecture 1's eigenvalues, doing something useful.

Turn the stretch up slowly. At 1 the rings are circles, the gradient points straight at the centre, and one step is enough. As the rings flatten the path starts to bounce, and by 20 it is crossing the valley almost at right angles, making almost no progress along it.

The figure uses the best fixed step there is, \(\alpha=2/(\lambda_{\min}+ \lambda_{\max})\), and that matters: this is not a badly tuned method, it is a well-tuned one showing its ceiling. With that step both coordinates are multiplied by \(\pm(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\) each time, and the sign flip on the stiff coordinate is the zig-zag. For \(\kappa=20\) the factor is \(0.905\), so a hundredfold reduction needs about forty-six steps.

Why this is not a curiosity: real problems are far worse than 20. The line fit of section II has \(\kappa\approx63\) as written, and the landscapes in lecture 4 are worse again. The next two slides stop paying this price without paying for the Hessian instead.

Load-bearing: momentum and Adam are both answers to this figure.

Momentum

Keep some of the last step.

$$v_{n+1}=\beta v_n-\alpha\nabla f(x_n)^{\mathsf T},\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+v_{n+1}$$

A velocity that remembers, rather than a step that forgets.

  • Crossing parts cancel, downhill parts add up
  • \(\beta=0\) is plain descent; close to 1 it overshoots

Speaker notes

The zig-zag wastes work in a very specific way: the across-the-valley part of each step is undone by the next one, while the along-the-valley part always points the same way. So add the steps up instead of taking them one at a time. The alternating parts cancel in the sum and the persistent part accumulates.

Run \(\beta\) up from zero on the narrow valley. At \(0\) the grey and coloured paths are the same. By \(0.3\) the crossing collapses after the first step and the path runs down the middle; after fourteen steps it is ten times closer to the bottom than plain descent. Keep going and around \(0.85\) it sails past the centre and loops back — too much memory, and the ball rolls up the far wall.

Then set \(\beta\) back to \(0.3\) and move \(\alpha\). It opens at \(0.091\), the best fixed step for plain descent here. Push it past \(0.1\) and the grey path blows up, as on the step-length slide, while the momentum path still converges: with momentum the ceiling rises from \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) to \(2(1+\beta)/\lambda_{\max}\). Pull it down towards \(0.01\) and both paths crawl, since memory cannot make up for steps that are too short.

The rolling-ball picture is worth one sentence and no more: \(v\) is a velocity, the gradient is a force, and \(\beta\) is what is left after friction. It is a good picture and a bad explanation, so do not lean on it.

Say where it goes: this is the standard addition to gradient descent in lecture 4, under this name and with \(\beta\) usually around \(0.9\). It costs one extra vector of memory and no extra gradients.

Adam

Momentum, and a step length for each coordinate.

$$m_{n+1}=\beta_1m_n+(1-\beta_1)\,g_n$$ $$s_{n+1}=\beta_2s_n+(1-\beta_2)\,g_n^2$$ $$x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha\,\frac{m_{n+1}}{\sqrt{s_{n+1}}+\varepsilon}$$

\(g_n=\nabla f(x_n)^{\mathsf T}\). Square, root and division per coordinate.

  • \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\), \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\)
  • \(m\) averages the gradient, \(s\) its square

Each coordinate moves about \(\alpha\) per step, however steep it is.

Speaker notes

The diagnosis from the zig-zag: one \(\alpha\) has to suit the stiffest direction, and every other direction then crawls. Adam gives each coordinate its own step length, and builds it from gradients it has already seen, so the Hessian is never needed.

Read the update one line at a time. \(m\) is momentum from the last slide, written as an average: nine parts old, one part new. \(s\) does the same for the squared gradient, per coordinate, with a much longer memory. The step divides one by the root of the other. For a coordinate whose gradient holds steady, \(m/\sqrt s\) is close to \(\pm1\) whatever its size, so every coordinate moves about \(\alpha\). A steep coordinate's large gradient is divided by its own size.

Run the figure at the default. The first step goes exactly \(\alpha\) in each coordinate, at 45 degrees, and the path crosses the valley once where plain descent crossed it on every step. Then point at the end: the path does not settle at the bottom, it hovers within about \(\alpha\) of it, because the step no longer shrinks with the gradient. In practice \(\alpha\) is lowered over the run for this reason.

One detail the slide leaves out. \(m\) and \(s\) start at zero, so early on they are too small. The published method divides them by \(1-\beta_1^n\) and \(1-\beta_2^n\) after \(n\) steps to correct for that, and the figure does too.

Where it goes: Adam is the usual default for training networks, and lecture 4 can use it as it stands. Cut candidate: if short, give the update and the claim and skip the slider.

Constrained optimization

The answer need not have zero gradient.

$$\min_x f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x)=0$$
$$\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g$$
  • The contour of \(f\) touches the constraint rather than crossing it
  • \(x^2+2y^2\) on \(x+y=\tfrac32\): the answer is \((1,\,\tfrac12)\)

Speaker notes

Until now \(x\) could be anything. Most real problems come with conditions: a budget, a fixed total, a quantity that cannot go negative.

The argument is geometric and takes one minute. Stand on the line. If the contour of \(f\) through that point crosses the line, then moving a little one way along the line goes to a lower contour, so the point is not the answer. Only where the contour is tangent to the line does no direction along the line go down. Tangency means the two normals are parallel, and the normals are the gradients: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\). The number \(\lambda\) is a Lagrange multiplier.

Check it on the figure with real numbers. At \((1,\,\tfrac12)\) the gradient of \(x^2+2y^2\) is \((2,\,2)\) and the gradient of \(x+y\) is \((1,\,1)\), so \(\lambda=2\). The unconstrained minimum is the origin, which is not on the line, and the constrained value is \(1.5\).

Inequalities in a sentence: for \(g(x)\le0\) either the answer is inside, where the constraint may be ignored, or on the boundary, where the equality case applies with a sign condition on \(\lambda\). Those are the KKT conditions. Named, not developed.

Stochastic gradient descent

Estimate the gradient from part of the sum.

$$f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_i,\qquad \nabla f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f_i \;\approx\;\frac{m}{\lvert B\rvert}\sum_{i\in B}\nabla f_i$$

One term per data point, \(m\) of them. The batch \(B\) is a few of the terms, a different few each step.

  • Each step costs \(\lvert B\rvert\) terms instead of \(m\)
  • The noise never dies: the path settles into a cloud

A worse gradient, taken often enough, gets there first.

Speaker notes

The last slide of the hour, and the one lecture 4 starts from. Every objective fitted today was a sum over data points, one term each, and so is the loss of a network. The figure is the line fit again: \(m=8\) points, and term \(i\) is \(\tfrac12(a+bu_i-y_i)^2\), with \(u\) measured from the middle of the data. A full gradient touches every term; with a million terms that is a million evaluations for one step.

But the sum has structure worth exploiting: each term contributes a vector, and a handful of terms, scaled up, estimates the total. The estimate is unbiased and noisy, and it costs a fraction as much.

The row of blocks is the eight terms. Pull the step slider back to zero and walk it forward: each step lights up the terms its batch used, and the path moves by their estimate alone. At batch 1 one block lights at a time; at batch 8 all of them do, and that is plain gradient descent.

The batch slider fixes the budget rather than the step count — sixty-four single terms every time — which is the comparison that matters, because terms are what gets paid for. At batch 8 the path is smooth and beautiful and only gets eight steps, ending far from the answer at \(f=5.4\). At batch 1 it is a drunkard's walk that takes sixty-four steps and ends at \(0.58\), against a best possible \(0.54\).

Point at the cloud at the end of the batch-1 path. The noise does not shrink as the answer is approached, so the path never settles — it orbits. Shrinking \(\alpha\) over the run shrinks the cloud, which is what a learning-rate schedule is for.

One last remark, then stop: the noise is not only a cost. A noisy step can leave a shallow dip that a clean gradient would have stayed in. On the landscapes of lecture 4 that is thought to be part of why it works.

Quiz

Five questions, hands up.

Speaker notes

Multiple choice, answered from the room. Read each one out, count hands for each letter, then give the answer. If time is short, ask 4 and 5: gradient descent and Adam are what lecture 4 trains with.

\(x^\star=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\;f(x)\) is

  1. AThe smallest value \(f\) takes
  2. BThe input where \(f\) is smallest
  3. CThe slope of \(f\) at its lowest point
  4. DThe largest value \(f\) takes

Speaker notes

Answer: B. The place, not the value: \(x^\star\) is an input. On the cubic of the minimization-problem slide, \(x^\star=1\), where \(f\) reaches \(0.5\).

A is the trap that slide warned about: \(0.5\) is the minimum, \(1\) is the minimizer, and optimization wants the place. C is always zero there, so it says nothing about where. D is a maximum, which is the same problem with \(-f\).

At the lowest point of a smooth curve, its slope is

  1. APositive
  2. BNegative
  3. CZero
  4. DAs steep as anywhere on the curve

Speaker notes

Answer: C. If the slope were positive, a small step left would go lower; if negative, a small step right would. Only a zero slope survives, which is why every method in the hour looks for \(f'(x)=0\).

A and B each name a direction that still goes down. D mixes up the slope with the curve's height. Follow-up worth asking: is every point with zero slope a lowest point? No: peaks and pauses have one too, as the stationary-points slide showed.

Compared with Newton's method, Gauss–Newton

  1. ANeeds no second derivatives
  2. BNeeds no derivatives at all
  3. CTakes the same step, but slower
  4. DWorks only in one dimension

Speaker notes

Answer: A. It replaces the Hessian by \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\), which is built from the Jacobian alone, first derivatives of the residuals. That is the whole point of linearizing the residual rather than the objective.

B goes too far: the Jacobian is still needed, and the code on the slide computes it. C has it backwards: the steps differ, because \(J^{\mathsf T}\!J\) is only part of the Hessian, and each one is cheaper. D is wrong on the slide itself, which fitted two parameters.

Each step of gradient descent moves in the direction of

  1. AThe gradient
  2. BThe level curve through the point
  3. CA straight line to the minimum
  4. DMinus the gradient

Speaker notes

Answer: D. The gradient points the way \(f\) rises fastest, so the step goes the opposite way: \(x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha\,\nabla f(x_n)^{\mathsf T}\).

A is uphill, the classic sign slip. B goes nowhere to first order: along the level curve \(f\) does not change, which was the point of the descent-directions slide. C is what one would like, and it is true only on a perfectly round bowl; on a stretched one the gradient points across the valley, which is the zig-zag.

What does Adam remember from earlier steps?

  1. ANothing: each step uses only the current gradient
  2. BRunning averages of the gradient and of its square
  3. CEvery point it has visited
  4. DThe Hessian

Speaker notes

Answer: B. \(m\) is the running average of the gradient, which is momentum, and \(s\) the running average of its square, which sets a step length per coordinate. Two vectors of memory, updated every step.

A is plain gradient descent, which forgets everything. C is far more than it keeps: it remembers no positions at all. D is what Adam is built to avoid; the squared gradients stand in for curvature it never computes.
