Vectors as geometric objects

An arrow. Where it is drawn carries no information.

A vector is a displacement: it says which way and how far. Two arrows represent the same vector when one is a translation of the other, so an arrow can be slid anywhere on the page without becoming a different vector.

A point and a vector get written the same way and are not the same thing. A point is where you are; a vector is how you move. Drawing every vector from the origin is a convenience that hides the difference, and this lecture uses it throughout.

Two arrows are the same vector when one is a translation of the other.

Speaker notes

Emphasise that the three arrows are one vector, not three. Ask which of them is "the" vector; the answer is that the question has no content.

Resist writing components here. They arrive on slide 6, and the two slides in between are deliberately coordinate-free so that the choice involved in coordinates is visible when it is made.

Addition, scalar multiplication and linear combinations

Two operations: adding arrows, and stretching them.

To add, put the tail of \(v\) at the head of \(u\); the sum is the arrow from start to finish. Doing it in the other order gives the other side of a parallelogram and the same answer, so addition does not care about order.

To multiply by a number \(c\), keep the direction and stretch by \(c\); a negative \(c\) reverses it. The multiples of a single nonzero vector fill a line through the origin.

$$a\,u+b\,v$$

Both operations at once: a linear combination of \(u\) and \(v\), with weights \(a\) and \(b\).

A linear combination is a weighted sum of vectors. Almost every object in this hour is one.

Speaker notes

Trace the parallelogram with a finger both ways round and say that the two routes end at the same place. That is the whole proof that \(u+v=v+u\).

Point at the two dots on their lines: the multiples of one vector fill a line. Slide 15 takes that observation and runs with it.

Coordinates in a basis

Two vectors to build from, and every other vector gets a pair of numbers.

Pick two vectors \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) that do not lie on one line. Every vector in the plane is then a linear combination of the two — and in exactly one way. Those two weights are the coordinates of \(v\) in that basis.

$$v=c_1b_1+c_2b_2,\qquad [v]_B=\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}$$

Two such vectors reach everything, so the weights exist; neither is a multiple of the other, so they are unique. Section III makes both halves precise.

Nothing asks \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) to meet at a right angle, or to agree with each other, or to be short. They carry a grid of their own, and the coordinates are read off that grid by completing a parallelogram.

Coordinates belong to the basis, not to the vector.

Speaker notes

Choose the basis on the figure with a finger before saying anything: two arrows, off a common line, and now the whole plane is addressed. Then walk the construction — along \(b_1\) until you can finish the trip on \(b_2\).

The coordinates \(1.75\) and \(0.75\) are deliberately unlovely. In the standard frame this same arrow is \((3,2)\), and neither pair is the true one. Existence and uniqueness are asserted here and proved on slides 15 and 16; say so rather than gliding over it.

Euclidean coordinates

The square grid, and why it is still a choice.

One kind of basis is worth singling out: the one where \(b_2\) is \(b_1\) given a quarter turn. The two then meet at a right angle and agree on what counts as one step, so the grid they draw is square. Coordinates read against such a frame are euclidean.

The standard basis \(e_1=(1,0)\), \(e_2=(0,1)\) is the one everybody draws, and \((3,1)\) means three steps across and one up. It is not the only euclidean frame: turn the whole thing and the angles are still right, the grid is still square, and the same arrow reads as different numbers.

$$[v]_E=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad [v]_Q=\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2\\-\sqrt2\end{bmatrix} \approx\begin{bmatrix}2.83\\-1.41\end{bmatrix}$$

A euclidean frame is square. It is still a choice, and turning it changes every coordinate.

Speaker notes

This is the title figure with its numbers filled in, and the thesis of the lecture: a computer holding \((3,1)\) is holding a description, not the arrow.

Where it goes: those green numbers are computed on slide 10 by projection, and slide 20 gives the matrix that converts one euclidean reading into the other. Everything from here to section III uses the standard frame by default — worth saying out loud, because it is an assumption, not a fact.

Orthogonality

Two directions at a right angle, and what that buys.

Two directions are orthogonal when they meet at a right angle. A euclidean frame is exactly a basis of two orthogonal vectors.

It matters for how a coordinate is found. In a skewed basis, reading \(c_1\) means drawing a line parallel to \(b_2\), so the two coordinates come out together or not at all. In an orthogonal basis it is a perpendicular drop, and the other axis contributes nothing.

Orthogonal vectors are also independent: neither can be built out of the other, since neither has any part lying along it.

In an orthogonal basis, each coordinate can be found on its own.

Speaker notes

Same arrow in both panels. Cover the right-hand one and ask how to read \(c_1\) on the left: people reach for a perpendicular and get the wrong answer. That mistake is the slide.

Right angles are the geometry. Slide 9 turns them into a number — the inner product, which is zero exactly when two vectors are orthogonal — and slide 10 turns the perpendicular drop into a formula.

The inner product: norm and angle

One number, from which length and angle both follow.

The inner product multiplies matching coordinates and adds. Its geometric meaning is the second line: length times length times the cosine of the angle between them.

$$u\cdot v=\sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i,\qquad u\cdot v=\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert\cos\theta$$

Length is the case \(v=u\): \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{u\cdot u}\), which is Pythagoras. The sum reads euclidean coordinates — a skewed basis needs different arithmetic for the same geometry.

With \(u=(3,1)\) and \(v=(1,2)\): \(u\cdot v=3+2=5\), \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{10}\), \(\lVert v\rVert=\sqrt5\), so \(\cos\theta=5/\sqrt{50}=1/\sqrt2\) and \(\theta=45^\circ\).

Length, angle and orthogonality are all read out of one number.

Speaker notes

The sign is the intuition worth keeping: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means at a right angle, negative means roughly opposite. In lecture 5 an attention score is exactly this number — a query dotted with a key — and it means the same thing there.

Do not prove that the two formulas agree; say it is the law of cosines rearranged. Do say the caveat in the panel out loud, since slide 8 has just made skewed bases real.

Orthogonal projection

Splitting a vector into a part along \(v\) and a part across it.

The projection of \(u\) onto \(v\) is the point on the line through \(v\) closest to \(u\): the perpendicular drop of slide 8, now with a formula.

$$p=\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v,\qquad r=u-p,\qquad r\cdot v=0$$

That coefficient is the only one making the residual orthogonal to \(v\), and \(p\) is then the closest point on the line.

With \(u=(3,1)\) and \(v=(1,1)\): \(u\cdot v=4\), \(v\cdot v=2\), so \(p=2v=(2,2)\) and \(r=(1,-1)\). Check: \(r\cdot v=1-1=0\).

Every vector splits, uniquely, into a part along \(v\) and a part orthogonal to \(v\).

Speaker notes

Say the word closest out loud, because that is the form this returns in: lecture 3 solves least squares, and the answer is the projection of the data onto what the model can reach.

Then close the loop with slide 7: \(p=(2,2)\) and \(r=(1,-1)\) are the two pieces of \(v=(3,1)\) along the turned frame's axes, and their sizes, \(2\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt2\), are precisely the green numbers there.

Systems of linear equations

Where do the lines meet?

An equation is linear when each unknown appears once, multiplied by a number, and the results are added — no powers, no products of unknowns. A system is several of them holding at once, and in two unknowns each one draws a line.

$$\begin{aligned}2x_1+x_2&=5\\x_1+2x_2&=4\end{aligned}$$

Both hold at \(x=(2,1)\), where the two lines cross.

The unknowns are placeholders. What the system carries is three arrays.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix}$$

A matrix starts life as the coefficients of a system.

Speaker notes

Two lines in the plane usually cross once. Ask what the exceptions are — parallel lines, no solution; the same line twice, infinitely many — and note that this is the entire theory of two-by-two systems, visible.

Say what is not in this lecture: how a system is actually solved. Elimination is the answer and it is a talk of its own; slide 23 names the factorization it produces. Today the system is here to force the notation.

The same system as \(Ax=b\)

One definition, chosen to make the notation say what it means.

We want \(Ax\) to stand for the left-hand sides of the system. That fixes the definition entirely: entry \(i\) of \(Ax\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) against \(x\).

$$(Ax)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}x_j \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad Ax=x_1a_1+\dots+x_na_n$$

By rows, a list of inner products. By columns, a combination of the columns of \(A\), weighted by the entries of \(x\).

Our system solves at \(x=(2,1)\) because

$$2\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+1\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix}=b$$

\(Ax\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\), with the entries of \(x\) as the weights.

Speaker notes

Both readings are needed later, so name them: the row picture is a list of inner products, the column picture is a linear combination. The column picture is the one that generalises — least squares in lecture 3 is the same sentence with \(b\) out of reach and the nearest combination taken instead.

Shapes: \((m\times n)\) times \((n\times1)\) gives \((m\times1)\). The inner numbers match and cancel. That rule is the whole of the next slide.

The matrix product and the transpose

What happens when one system feeds into another.

Suppose \(x\) is itself produced by a system, \(x=Bz\), and we want \(y=Ax\). Writing out \(A(Bz)\) shows a single matrix does the whole job.

$$(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik}B_{kj},\qquad (m\times n)(n\times p)=(m\times p)$$

Entry \((i,j)\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) against column \(j\) of \(B\). The inner dimensions are what the sum runs over.

$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\; S=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}:\quad RS=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\2&0\end{bmatrix}\neq \begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=SR$$

The transpose flips the array about its diagonal, \((A^{\mathsf T})_{ij}=A_{ji}\). It reverses products and writes the inner product as one: \(u\cdot v=u^{\mathsf T}v\).

\(AB\) is the single matrix that does \(B\) first and then \(A\). Which is why the order cannot be swapped.

Speaker notes

Do \(RS\) and \(SR\) on the board if the room wants it; they are four multiplications each. Do not try to make them mean anything yet — these two matrices come back on slide 17 as pictures, and that is where non-commutativity stops being an algebraic accident.

The transpose is here only because the notation is needed later: orthogonal matrices on slide 19, symmetric ones on slide 22. Do not dwell.

The identity matrix and the inverse

The system that changes nothing, and the system run backwards.

The identity \(I\) has ones down the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. As a system it says \(x_1=b_1\), \(x_2=b_2\): it asks nothing. And \(Ix=x\), \(AI=IA=A\), so it is the \(1\) of matrix multiplication.

A square \(A\) is invertible when some \(A^{-1}\) undoes it. Then the system has exactly one solution and we can write it down.

$$A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad Ax=b\iff x=A^{-1}b$$
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix} =\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}6\\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Not every matrix has one. In \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) the second equation says nothing the first did not, and there is no way back.

\(A^{-1}\) is the system run backwards.

Speaker notes

Worth saying once, plainly: writing \(x=A^{-1}b\) is not how a system gets solved in practice. Forming the inverse is slower and less accurate than eliminating, and slide 23 names what software does instead. The inverse is a statement about existence and uniqueness, not an algorithm.

Check the arithmetic out loud — \(\tfrac13(2\cdot5-1\cdot4)=2\), \(\tfrac13(-5+8)=1\) — and note it agrees with the crossing point on slide 11.

Span and linear independence

What a set of vectors can reach, and whether any of them is spare.

The span of a set of vectors is the set of all their linear combinations: everything reachable by scaling and adding. One nonzero vector spans a line through the origin; two span a plane, unless they lie on the same line.

$$\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}=\{c_1v_1+\dots+c_kv_k\;:\;c_i\in\mathbb{R}\}$$

They are linearly independent when the only combination equal to zero has all coefficients zero — equivalently, none of them lies in the span of the others. So \((1,2)\) and \((2,4)\) are dependent: \(2(1,2)-(2,4)=0\), and the second adds nothing.

Dependence means redundancy, detected by a combination that vanishes.

Speaker notes

The two figures are the slide. In the right-hand one, ask how to reach a point off the line: nothing you do with those two vectors gets there. Those are exactly the columns of the matrix with no inverse on slide 14.

Cut candidate: this folds into the next slide if time is tight — state span and independence in two sentences on the way to defining a basis.

Basis and dimension

Enough vectors to reach everything, and not one more.

A basis of a space is a set of vectors that is both independent and spanning. The two conditions pull in opposite directions and meet exactly where coordinates work:

  • Spanning gives existence — every vector can be written as a combination of the basis.
  • Independence gives uniqueness — it can be written that way in only one way.

Every basis of a space has the same number of vectors; that number is the dimension. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) it is \(n\): any \(n\) independent vectors form a basis, and any \(n+1\) are dependent.

A basis is precisely what is needed for coordinates to exist and to be unique.

Speaker notes

Uniqueness is the half people skip, and it is the half that matters: if a vector had two different coordinate lists, the word "coordinates" would mean nothing. One line of proof if the room is willing — two lists subtract to a vanishing combination, so independence forces them to agree.

Dimension in the wild: a word embedding in lecture 5 lives in \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\) or thereabouts. Nothing changes except that nobody draws the picture.

Matrices as linear transformations

The array was never the point. The map is.

Stop asking which \(x\) solves \(Ax=b\); ask where \(Ax\) goes as \(x\) ranges over everything. Then \(A\) is a linear map: it respects both operations of slide 5.

$$T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),\qquad T(cu)=c\,T(u)$$

Every matrix gives such a map, and every such map is a matrix.

That forces a great deal: the origin cannot move, lines stay straight and parallel, spacing stays even. And \(AB\) now has a picture — slide 13's \(R\) is a quarter turn, its \(S\) doubles the horizontal axis.

A square grid becomes a grid of identical parallelograms. That is all a linear map may do.

Grey is the figure before the map; the thin lines are the image of a square grid.

Speaker notes

This is the turn of the lecture, and the first time the logo appears. Read the two panels out loud: stretch then turn gives a shape standing up, turn then stretch gives one lying down. Same two operations, different results, and no algebra needed to see it — that is slide 13's inequality, in pictures.

The negative statements are the useful ones: a linear map cannot bend a line and cannot move the origin. Lecture 4's first real claim — that a stack of linear layers collapses into one — is this slide and nothing more.

The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors

Choose where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) go, and the matrix is written.

Let \(e_1=(1,0)\) and \(e_2=(0,1)\). By the column reading of slide 12, \(Ae_1\) picks out the first column of \(A\) and \(Ae_2\) the second: the columns are where the basis vectors land.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}|&|\\Ae_1&Ae_2\\|&|\end{bmatrix},\qquad Ax=x_1\,Ae_1+x_2\,Ae_2$$

And that is everything, because every \(x\) is a combination of \(e_1\) and \(e_2\). To find the matrix of a transformation, apply it to the basis vectors and write the answers as columns.

A linear map is determined by what it does to a basis.

Speaker notes

The load-bearing slide of the lecture. Drag one arrow onto the other and watch the figure collapse onto a line: the two columns are now dependent, the map has thrown away a whole direction, and no inverse can exist. That is slide 14's \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\), live.

Then read the matrix off the two arrows out loud, once, slowly. If the room follows that, the rest of the section is bookkeeping.

Rotation, scaling, shear, reflection

Four families of matrix, and one control that is not a matrix at all.

$$R_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix} \quad S=\begin{bmatrix}s_1&0\\0&s_2\end{bmatrix} \quad H_k=\begin{bmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$$

Each is read off the previous slide: the columns of \(R_\theta\) are \(e_1,e_2\) turned by \(\theta\), and \(H_k\) slides \(e_2\) sideways. Reflection in the line at angle \(\varphi\) is \(F_\varphi=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\cos2\varphi&\sin2\varphi\\\sin2\varphi&-\cos2\varphi\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\).

A matrix is orthogonal when \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\): unit columns at right angles. Then \((Qu)\cdot(Qv)=u^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qv=u\cdot v\), so every length and angle survives. Turns and flips are orthogonal; shears and scalings are not. The determinant is the factor areas scale by, and at zero the figure is flat with nothing to bring it back.

Translation is the odd one out. It shifts the origin, which no linear map may do. Appending a coordinate equal to \(1\) makes it a matrix again — how lecture 4 folds in a bias term.

Speaker notes

Sweep the rotation slider through a full turn: grey and red stay congruent, which is orthogonality, and the determinant sits at \(1\) throughout. Switch to shear and sweep: the figure leans, the horizontal axis is untouched, and the determinant is still \(1\) although lengths have obviously changed. Area preserved is not shape preserved.

Then take scaling and drive \(s_2\) to its floor. Watch the figure flatten and the determinant approach zero — the collapse of slide 18, with a number attached. Finally switch to translation and note the readout has grown a row: the matrix is three by three, because translation was never a two-by-two map.

Change of basis

A matrix that translates between two descriptions.

Slide 7 left a question open: what converts one list of coordinates into the other? Collect the new basis vectors as the columns of \(B\). Then \(B\) rebuilds a vector from its coordinates, and \(B^{-1}\) goes back.

$$v=B\,[v]_B,\qquad [v]_B=B^{-1}v$$

For an orthonormal basis the inverse is free: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) says \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\), so every coordinate is a projection.

$$[v]_Q=Q^{\mathsf T}v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2\\-\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}$$

A map changes too: if \(y=Ax\) in the old coordinates, the same map is \(B^{-1}AB\) in the new ones.

\(B\) takes coordinates to vectors, \(B^{-1}\) the other way.

Speaker notes

The direction of the arrows is what everyone gets backwards, lecturer included. Say it as a sentence and lean on it: the columns of \(B\) are the new basis written in the old one, so \(B\) must be the map that consumes new coordinates and produces old ones.

End on \(B^{-1}AB\) and pose the next slide as a question: if we may choose any basis we like, is there one that makes \(A\) as simple as possible?

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The directions a map does not turn.

Almost every vector comes out of \(A\) pointing somewhere else. A nonzero \(v\) that does not is an eigenvector: the map leaves its direction alone and only scales it, by the eigenvalue \(\lambda\).

$$Av=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq0$$

Rearranged, \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) with \(v\neq0\), so \(A-\lambda I\) must flatten something onto zero: the \(\lambda\) that work are the roots of \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}:\quad \lambda_1=3,\;v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix};\qquad \lambda_2=1,\;v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Two caveats, named rather than developed: \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) has only one eigendirection instead of two, and a rotation has no real eigenvector at all — it turns everything.

An eigenvector is a direction the map only stretches.

Speaker notes

Check \(A(1,1)=(3,3)\) and \(A(-1,1)=(-1,1)\) out loud — four additions, and it settles what the definition means. Note the two eigendirections are perpendicular here; that is not luck, it is because \(A\) is symmetric, and the next slide uses it.

Do not compute the characteristic polynomial unless asked. Where eigenvalues go: in lecture 3 the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian is the condition number, and it predicts how badly steepest descent zig-zags.

Eigendecomposition

Turn, stretch, turn back.

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of \(B\). Applying \(A\) scales each one, so \(AB=B\Lambda\) with \(\Lambda\) diagonal — slide 20 with the basis chosen well.

$$B^{-1}AB=\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&\lambda_2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad A=B\Lambda B^{-1}$$

In eigen-coordinates the map only stretches each axis. When \(A\) is symmetric the eigenvectors are orthogonal, so \(B^{-1}\) is just \(Q^{\mathsf T}\).

$$\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} =Q\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}Q^{\mathsf T},\qquad Q=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$$

Read right to left, that is the slider: turn by \(-45^\circ\), stretch by \(3\) and \(1\), turn back.

A decomposition is a change of basis in which the map becomes simple.

Speaker notes

Drag slowly and name each stage as it happens: turn, stretch, turn back. Stop at stage two and point out that the stretching is along the coordinate axes there — that is the entire reason for the first turn.

What it buys: \(A^k=B\Lambda^kB^{-1}\), so a hundred applications of \(A\) cost one diagonal power. Repeated application is what makes eigenvalues turn up in stability, in growth rates, and in lecture 3's condition number.

LU and the SVD, in brief

Two more factorizations, named rather than developed.

LU. \(A=LU\), with \(L\) lower triangular and \(U\) upper triangular — elimination, recorded. Triangular systems are solved by substitution from one end, so one factorization solves \(Ax=b\) for any number of right-hand sides. This, not \(A^{-1}\), is what software does.

SVD. Unlike the last slide it asks for nothing: every matrix factors this way, square or not.

$$A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T},\qquad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1\!\ge\!\sigma_2\!\ge\!\dots\!\ge\!0)$$

Turn, stretch by the singular values, turn again: the unit circle always becomes an ellipse.

The shear of slide 21, which had no second eigendirection, has \(\sigma_1=1.618\) and \(\sigma_2=0.618\) — product \(1\), since a shear preserves area. Dropping the small \(\sigma_i\) gives the best low-rank approximation, which is what principal component analysis is.

Speaker notes

Deliberately quick, and the first thing to cut. The one sentence to land: every matrix, without exception, is a turn followed by a stretch along axes followed by a turn — so an ellipse is the most a linear map can make of a circle.

Pay off slide 14 explicitly here: LU is the honest answer to "how is \(Ax=b\) actually solved". And flag \(\sigma_1/\sigma_n\) as lecture 3's condition number.

Quiz

Five questions, answers from the room.

  1. \(u=(2,-1)\) and \(v=(1,2)\). What is \(u\cdot v\), and what is the angle between them?
  2. Write \(3x_1-x_2=1\), \(x_1+x_2=3\) as \(Ax=b\). Which combination of the columns of \(A\) gives \(b\)?
  3. A linear map sends \(e_1\) to \((0,1)\) and \(e_2\) to \((-1,0)\). Write down its matrix. What map is it?
  4. Do \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)? What do they span, and does the matrix with those columns have an inverse?
  5. \(A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) has \((1,1)\) as an eigenvector. What is the eigenvalue, and what is the other eigenvector?

Speaker notes

1. \(2-2=0\), so they are orthogonal: \(90^\circ\). 2. \(A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}3&-1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\), \(b=(1,3)\), and \(x=(1,2)\): one of the first column plus two of the second. 3. Columns are the images, so \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) — a quarter turn counterclockwise, the \(R\) of slides 13 and 17. 4. They span the line through \((1,1)\), and the matrix flattens the plane onto it, so there is no inverse. 5. \(A(1,1)=(3,3)\), so \(\lambda=3\); the other is \((-1,1)\) with \(\lambda=1\), perpendicular to the first because \(A\) is symmetric.
