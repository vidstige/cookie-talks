Vectors as geometric objects

A displacement, before it is a list of numbers.

A vector is a displacement: a direction together with a length. Two arrows represent the same vector when one is a translation of the other — where an arrow is drawn carries no information.

Choose axes and a unit, and every vector acquires a list of numbers, its components: how far along each axis. We write them stacked, and the set of them is \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

$$v=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad v=\begin{bmatrix}v_1\\\vdots\\v_n\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^n$$

The vector is the object. Its components are a description, and they depend on a choice.

Speaker notes

Emphasise that the three arrows in the figure are the same vector, and that a point and a vector are different things that get written the same way: a point is where you are, a vector is how you move. In this lecture we mostly identify them by taking the origin as the start of every arrow, which is a convenience, not a fact.

If someone asks about column versus row: it is a convention, and it will start mattering on slide 9 when we transpose.

Addition, scalar multiplication and linear combinations

Two operations, and everything in the lecture is built from them.

To add, put the tail of \(v\) at the head of \(u\); the sum is the arrow from start to finish. Doing it in the other order gives the other side of a parallelogram and the same result. In components, add entrywise.

To multiply by a number \(c\), keep the direction and scale the length by \(c\); a negative \(c\) reverses it. Combining both operations gives a linear combination.

$$u+v=\begin{bmatrix}u_1+v_1\\u_2+v_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad cu=\begin{bmatrix}cu_1\\cu_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad a\,u+b\,v$$

A linear combination is a weighted sum of vectors. Almost every object in this lecture is one.

Speaker notes

Worth doing on the board: \(u=(3,1)\), \(v=(1,2)\), so \(u+v=(4,3)\) — then point at the figure. The scalar multiples of a single vector fill a line through the origin; that observation is the whole of slide 14.

Cut if short on time: the second worked sum. The figure carries it.

The inner product: norm and angle

One number, from which length and angle both follow.

The inner product multiplies matching components and adds. Its geometric meaning is the second line: length times length times the cosine of the angle between them.

$$u\cdot v=\sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i,\qquad u\cdot v=\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert\cos\theta$$

Length is the case \(v=u\): \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{u\cdot u}\), which is Pythagoras. The second equality then gives the angle.

With \(u=(3,1)\) and \(v=(1,2)\): \(u\cdot v=3+2=5\), \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{10}\), \(\lVert v\rVert=\sqrt5\), so \(\cos\theta=5/\sqrt{50}=1/\sqrt2\) and \(\theta=45^\circ\).

Length and angle are not extra structure. They are read out of the inner product.

Speaker notes

The sign is the intuition to leave behind: positive means roughly the same direction, zero means perpendicular, negative means roughly opposite. In lecture 5 the attention scores are exactly this number — a query dotted with a key — and they mean the same thing there: how much do these two point the same way.

Do not prove the equality of the two formulas. Say it is the law of cosines rearranged and continue.

Orthogonality and orthogonal projection

Splitting a vector into a part along \(v\) and a part across it.

Two nonzero vectors are orthogonal when \(u\cdot v=0\), since then \(\cos\theta=0\). The projection of \(u\) onto \(v\) is the point on the line through \(v\) closest to \(u\): drop a perpendicular from the head of \(u\) to that line.

$$p=\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v,\qquad r=u-p,\qquad r\cdot v=0$$

The coefficient is the only one that makes the residual orthogonal to \(v\), and \(p\) is the closest point on the line to \(u\).

With \(u=(3,1)\), \(v=(1,1)\): \(u\cdot v=4\), \(v\cdot v=2\), so \(p=2v=(2,2)\) and \(r=(1,-1)\). Check: \(r\cdot v=1-1=0\).

Every vector splits, uniquely, into a part along \(v\) and a part orthogonal to \(v\).

Speaker notes

Say the word closest out loud, because that is the form this slide returns in: lecture 3 solves least squares, and the answer is the projection of the data onto the space the model can reach. Also flag slide 16: in a basis of perpendicular unit vectors, coordinates are just projections, one dot product per axis.

If time is short, drop the formula's derivation entirely and keep the worked numbers — they are clean by construction.

Matrices and the matrix–vector product

An array of numbers, and the one operation that gives it meaning.

An \(m\times n\) matrix is a rectangular array, with entry \(A_{ij}\) in row \(i\) and column \(j\). Multiplying it by a vector of \(n\) entries gives one of \(m\) entries, and there are two ways to read the definition.

$$(Ax)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}x_j \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad Ax=x_1a_1+\dots+x_na_n$$

Row \(i\) of \(A\) dotted with \(x\); or the columns \(a_1,\dots,a_n\) of \(A\) combined with the entries of \(x\) as weights.

$$\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\end{bmatrix} =1\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$$

\(Ax\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\), with the entries of \(x\) as the weights.

Speaker notes

Both readings are needed later, so give both a name: the row picture is a list of dot products, the column picture is a linear combination. Slide 19 solves a system by looking at each in turn, and slide 20 defines rank from the column picture.

Shapes: \((m\times n)\) times \((n\times 1)\) gives \((m\times 1)\). The inner numbers have to match and they cancel. That rule is the whole of the next slide.

The matrix product and the transpose

Rows against columns; and why order matters.

For \(A\) of shape \(m\times n\) and \(B\) of shape \(n\times p\), the product \(AB\) is \(m\times p\), and entry \((i,j)\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) dotted with column \(j\) of \(B\). Equivalently, column \(j\) of \(AB\) is \(A\) applied to column \(j\) of \(B\).

$$(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik}B_{kj}\qquad \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&2&1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}2&2&-1\\1&4&1\end{bmatrix}$$

The transpose flips the array about its diagonal, \((A^{\mathsf T})_{ij}=A_{ji}\), which turns columns into rows. It reverses products, \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\), and it writes the inner product as a matrix product, \(u\cdot v=u^{\mathsf T}v\).

\(AB\) means: apply \(B\), then \(A\). Which is why \(AB\neq BA\) in general.

$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad S=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$$ $$RS=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\2&0\end{bmatrix}\neq \begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=SR$$

Speaker notes

Read the right-hand column out loud: \(R\) is a quarter turn, \(S\) stretches the horizontal axis by two. Stretch then turn gives a shape standing up; turn then stretch gives one lying down. Same two operations, different results, and no algebra needed to see it.

The transpose is on this slide only because we need the notation later — orthogonal matrices on slide 12 and symmetric ones on slide 21. Do not dwell.

Matrices as linear transformations

The array was never the point. The map is.

A map \(T\) is linear when it respects both operations of slide 5. Every linear map is a matrix product and every matrix product is a linear map: the two notions coincide.

$$T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),\qquad T(cu)=c\,T(u)$$

Together: \(T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)\).

Geometrically that forces a great deal: the origin cannot move, straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, and equally spaced points stay equally spaced. A square grid maps to a grid of identical parallelograms.

Grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put. That is all a linear map is allowed to do.

Speaker notes

The negative statements are the useful ones: a linear map cannot bend a line, cannot move the origin, and cannot translate. Translation is the subject of slide 13, and lecture 4's first real claim — that a stack of linear layers collapses to one — is this slide plus the previous one.

Demonstration: ask what shape the unit square becomes. Answer: a parallelogram, spanned by the two columns. That is the next slide.

The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors

Choose where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) go, and the matrix is written.

Let \(e_1=(1,0)\) and \(e_2=(0,1)\). By the column picture of slide 8, \(Ae_1\) selects the first column and \(Ae_2\) the second: the columns of \(A\) are where the basis vectors land.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}|&|\\Ae_1&Ae_2\\|&|\end{bmatrix},\qquad Ax=x_1\,Ae_1+x_2\,Ae_2$$

That determines everything: to find the matrix of a transformation, apply it to the basis vectors and write the answers as columns.

A linear map is determined by what it does to a basis. The matrix is that record, nothing more.

This is the load-bearing slide of the lecture. Drag one arrow onto the other and watch the cloud collapse to a line — that is the determinant going to zero, and it is slide 20's rank deficiency seen in advance. Drag one arrow past the other and the determinant changes sign: the plane has been flipped over.

Rotation, scaling, shear, reflection; orthogonal matrices

Four families, and the property that singles out two of them.

$$R_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix} \quad S=\begin{bmatrix}s_1&0\\0&s_2\end{bmatrix}$$ $$H_k=\begin{bmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \quad F_\varphi=\begin{bmatrix}\cos2\varphi&\sin2\varphi\\\sin2\varphi&-\cos2\varphi\end{bmatrix}$$

Each is read off the previous slide: the columns of \(R_\theta\) are \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) turned by \(\theta\), and \(H_k\) slides \(e_2\) sideways while leaving \(e_1\) alone.

A matrix is orthogonal when \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\): its columns are unit vectors, pairwise orthogonal. Then \((Qu)\cdot(Qv)=u^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qv=u\cdot v\), so every length and angle survives. Rotations and reflections are orthogonal; shears and scalings are not.

Orthogonal means rigid: only turning and flipping remain.

Speaker notes

Sweep the rotation slider through a full turn and note that the grey and red clouds stay congruent — nothing is distorted, which is orthogonality. Then switch to shear and sweep: the cloud leans, the horizontal axis is untouched, and the determinant stays at one although lengths clearly changed. Area preserved is not the same as shape preserved.

Reflection is worth one moment: sweep \(\varphi\) and watch the determinant sit at \(-1\) throughout. Cut candidate if the room is quick — the slide survives on rotation and shear alone.

Affine maps and homogeneous coordinates

Translation is not linear. One extra coordinate repairs that.

An affine map is a linear map followed by a translation, \(x\mapsto Ax+t\). It is not linear: it moves the origin to \(t\), and no \(2\times2\) matrix can do that.

Append a coordinate equal to \(1\), and composition is matrix multiplication again: the block matrix below sends \((x_1,x_2,1)\) to \((Ax+t,\,1)\).

$$\begin{bmatrix}A&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}Ax+t\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

An affine map in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a linear map in \(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\) that fixes the last coordinate.

Speaker notes

Move only the translation sliders first and let the room see the origin leave the crossing of the axes; that is the failure of linearity, visibly. Then note that the rotation acts before the translation, so turning the angle swings the cloud about the origin and not about its own centre.

Forward reference to pay off in lecture 4: a layer of a neural network computes \(Wx+b\), an affine map, and the trick on this slide is exactly why the bias can be folded into the weight matrix as one extra column.

Span and linear independence

What a set of vectors can reach, and whether any of them is spare.

The span of a set of vectors is the set of all their linear combinations: everything reachable by scaling and adding. One nonzero vector spans a line through the origin; two span a plane, unless they lie on the same line.

$$\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}=\{c_1v_1+\dots+c_kv_k\;:\;c_i\in\mathbb{R}\}$$

They are linearly independent when the only combination equal to zero has all coefficients zero — equivalently, none of them lies in the span of the others. So \((1,2)\) and \((2,4)\) are dependent: \(2(1,2)-(2,4)=0\), and the second adds nothing.

Dependence means redundancy, detected by a combination that vanishes.

Speaker notes

The two figures are the slide. In the right-hand one, ask what happens if you want to reach a point off the line: nothing you do with those two vectors gets there. That is the picture behind "no solution" on slide 20.

Cut candidate: this slide folds into the next one if time is tight — state span and independence in two sentences on the way to defining a basis.

Basis and dimension

Enough vectors to reach everything, and not one more.

A basis of a space is a set of vectors that is both independent and spanning. The two conditions pull in opposite directions and meet exactly where coordinates work:

  • Spanning gives existence — every vector can be written as a combination of the basis.
  • Independence gives uniqueness — it can be written that way in only one way.

Every basis of a space has the same number of vectors; that number is the dimension. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) it is \(n\): any \(n\) independent vectors form a basis, and any \(n+1\) are dependent.

A basis is precisely what is needed for coordinates to exist and to be unique.

Speaker notes

Uniqueness is the half people skip, and it is the half that matters: if a vector had two different coordinate representations, the word "coordinates" would be meaningless. Prove it in one line if the room is willing — two representations subtract to a vanishing combination, so independence forces the coefficients to agree.

Dimension in the wild: a word embedding in lecture 5 lives in \(\mathbb{R}^{768}\) or thereabouts. Nothing changes except that no one draws the picture.

Coordinates relative to a basis; orthonormal bases

The same vector, two lists of numbers.

Given a basis \(B=\{b_1,b_2\}\), the coordinates of \(v\) are the unique weights that rebuild it — found by solving a linear system.

$$v=c_1b_1+c_2b_2,\qquad [v]_B=\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}$$

Unless the basis is orthonormal: unit vectors, mutually orthogonal. Then dotting with \(q_i\) kills every term but one, and each coordinate is a projection — one dot product per axis.

$$c_i=v\cdot q_i,\qquad q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\; q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix},\; [v]_Q=\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2\\-\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}$$

Coordinates belong to the basis, not to the vector.

Speaker notes

Check the arithmetic on the board, because it closes the loop with slide 7: \(2\sqrt2\,q_1=(2,2)\) and \(-\sqrt2\,q_2=(1,-1)\), and those add to \((3,1)\). Those are the very two pieces the projection example produced.

This is the figure to point at when someone asks why any of this matters: two different pairs of numbers, one vector, and neither pair is more correct. Orthonormal bases return in lecture 5, where each attention head projects into its own low-dimensional frame.

Change of basis

A matrix that translates between two descriptions.

Collect the basis vectors as the columns of \(B\). Then \(B\) applied to a coordinate vector rebuilds the vector — the column picture again — and the inverse direction is \(B^{-1}\).

$$v=B\,[v]_B,\qquad [v]_B=B^{-1}v$$

\(B\) is invertible exactly because its columns are a basis.

For an orthonormal basis the inverse is free: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) says \(Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}\), whose rows dot the vector with each \(q_i\) — the previous slide's projection, as a matrix.

$$[v]_Q=Q^{\mathsf T}v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}2\sqrt2\\-\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}$$

\(B\) takes coordinates to vectors; \(B^{-1}\) takes vectors to coordinates.

Speaker notes

The direction of the arrows is the thing everyone gets backwards, including the lecturer. Say it as a sentence and rely on it: the columns of \(B\) are the new basis written in the old one, so \(B\) must be the map that consumes new coordinates and produces old ones.

Verify the transpose identity for the example numerically if asked; it is one multiplication. Then move on, because the next slide is the payoff.

Similar matrices

One map, two matrices, related by a change of basis.

Let \(y=Ax\) in the standard basis and let \(B\) hold a new basis. In the new coordinates the same map is \(B^{-1}AB\): read in, apply \(A\), read back.

$$[y]_B=B^{-1}A\,B\,[x]_B,\qquad A'=B^{-1}AB$$

\(A\) and \(A'\) are similar: one transformation from two points of view. Trace, determinant, rank and eigenvalues agree.

With \(A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) and \(Q\) the rotated basis of slide 16, the off-diagonal entries vanish:

$$Q^{\mathsf T}AQ=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$$

In the right basis this map only stretches: by \(3\) along one axis, by \(1\) along the other.

Trace the square with a finger, twice, and say the sentence: going around the outside is the same as going across the middle. Then flag the forward reference explicitly — finding a basis in which a matrix becomes diagonal is the whole of slide 21, and we have just done it once by hand.

Systems of linear equations

The same question asked of rows and of columns.

A system of \(m\) linear equations in \(n\) unknowns is one matrix equation \(Ax=b\). The two readings of \(Ax\) from slide 8 give two pictures of it, and they answer different questions.

$$\begin{aligned}2x_1+x_2&=5\\x_1+2x_2&=4\end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix}$$
  • Row picture. Each equation is a line; a solution is a point on every line at once, so the solution set is their intersection.
  • Column picture. Which combination of the columns produces \(b\)? Here \(2(2,1)+1(1,2)=(5,4)\), so \(x=(2,1)\).

\(Ax=b\) asks whether \(b\) is in the span of the columns of \(A\), and if so, with which weights.

Speaker notes

Say plainly what is not covered: how a system is actually solved. Gaussian elimination and the LU, QR and Cholesky factorizations are the standard machinery, and they are a talk of their own. Here we only need to know what the equation means and when it has an answer.

The column picture is the one to insist on, because it generalises: the same sentence describes least squares in lecture 3, where \(b\) is not in the span and the best available combination is taken instead.

Rank, null space, existence and uniqueness

What the map reaches, and what it destroys.

The column space of \(A\) is the span of its columns — everything \(Ax\) can reach — and its dimension is the rank. The null space is everything sent to zero. The two answer the two questions one can ask of \(Ax=b\):

  • Existence. A solution exists exactly when \(b\) lies in the column space.
  • Uniqueness. It is unique exactly when the null space is \(\{0\}\); otherwise adding any null vector gives another solution.
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}:\quad \operatorname{rank}=1,\quad \text{col space}=\operatorname{span}\!\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\quad \text{null space}=\operatorname{span}\!\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}$$

Solutions form a particular solution plus the whole null space. Rank counts what survives; the null space is what was lost.

Speaker notes

Tie it back to slide 11's demonstration: dragging the two arrows onto one line is precisely this matrix. Once the cloud is flat, the map cannot be undone — information is gone, not merely hidden.

Forward reference: lecture 3's condition number measures how close a matrix is to this situation, and it is why an optimizer can crawl. Nearly singular behaves like singular in floating point.

Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and diagonalization

The directions a map does not turn.

A nonzero \(v\) with \(Av=\lambda v\) is an eigenvector: the map leaves its direction alone and only scales it, by the eigenvalue \(\lambda\), a root of \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}:\quad \lambda_1=3,\;v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix};\qquad \lambda_2=1,\;v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of \(B\) and this is slide 18 with the basis chosen well: \(B^{-1}AB=\Lambda\) is diagonal, so \(A=B\Lambda B^{-1}\). Symmetric matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors, giving \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}\).

Two caveats, named rather than developed: \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) is not diagonalizable, and a rotation has no real eigenvector at all.

Diagonalizing is choosing a basis in which the map only stretches.

Speaker notes

Point at the two dashed lines and note that they are the only two directions with that property; everything else in the cloud gets turned as well as stretched. Then say what eigenvalues are for downstream: in lecture 3 the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian is the condition number, and it predicts the zig-zag of steepest descent.

Do not compute the characteristic polynomial unless asked. Determinants are deliberately a remark in this series.

The singular value decomposition

Every matrix is a rotation, a scaling along axes, and a rotation.

Eigenvectors need a square matrix and may not exist. The singular value decomposition has no conditions: every matrix factors as

$$A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T},\qquad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1\!\ge\!\sigma_2\!\ge\!\dots\!\ge\!0)$$

with \(U\), \(V\) orthogonal. Read right to left: turn, stretch along the axes by the singular values, turn again.

So the unit circle always maps to an ellipse, with semi-axes \(\sigma_i\) along the columns of \(U\). The shear \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) of slide 21, which had no second eigenvector, factors with \(\sigma_1=1.618\) and \(\sigma_2=0.618\); their product is \(1\), since a shear preserves area.

The rank is the number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\), the ratio \(\sigma_1/\sigma_n\) is lecture 3's condition number, and dropping small \(\sigma_i\) gives the best low-rank approximation.

Speaker notes

Drag the slider slowly and name each stage as it happens: turn, stretch, turn. Stop at stage two and point out that the ellipse axes are aligned with the coordinate axes there, and that the last rotation only carries them somewhere else. That is the whole content of the theorem.

Worth saying once: this is the decomposition behind low-rank approximation and principal components, and it is the reason people say a matrix "is" a rotation, a scaling and a rotation. If time is gone, show the endpoints only.
