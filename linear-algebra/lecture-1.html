Vectors as geometric objects

A displacement: which way, and how far.

  • Same direction, same length ⇒ same vector
  • Where it is drawn carries no information
  • A point is where you are; a vector is how you move

Two arrows are the same vector when one is a translation of the other.

Speaker notes

Emphasise that the three arrows are one vector, not three. Ask which of them is "the" vector; the answer is that the question has no content.

A point and a vector get written the same way and are not the same thing. Drawing every vector from the origin is a convenience that hides the difference, and this lecture uses it throughout.

Resist writing components here. They arrive two slides on, and the slides in between are deliberately coordinate-free so that the choice involved in coordinates is visible when it is made.

Addition, scalar multiplication and linear combinations

  • Add vectors \(u + v\). Tail to head
  • Scale by a scalar \(a\). The arrow becomes \(a\) times longer
  • Linear combination: \(a\,u + b\,v\)

Speaker notes

To add, put the tail of \(v\) at the head of \(u\); the sum is the arrow from start to finish. Trace the parallelogram with a finger both ways round and say the two routes end at the same place. That is the whole proof that \(u+v=v+u\).

Scaling: keep the direction, stretch by \(c\); a negative \(c\) reverses it. Point at the two dots on their lines — the multiples of one nonzero vector fill a line through the origin. That is worth a beat, because it is why a basis needs two vectors off a common line to reach the whole plane.

Coordinates in a basis

Pick \(b_1, b_2\) not on one line. Then every \(v\) is a combination of the two, in exactly one way:

$$v=c_1b_1+c_2b_2$$

\(c_1, c_2\) are the coordinates of \(v\) in that basis.

Coordinates belong to the basis, not to the vector.

Speaker notes

Choose the basis on the figure by dragging, before saying anything: two arrows, off a common line, and now the whole plane is addressed. Then walk the construction — along \(b_1\) until you can finish the trip on \(b_2\).

The demonstration to run: leave \(v\) alone and drag the basis vectors. The red arrow never moves, but the two numbers change continuously. That is the claim at the bottom of the slide, live. Then drag \(b_2\) onto \(b_1\) and watch the coordinates blow up as the basis stops being one.

Nothing asks \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) to meet at a right angle or to be the same length. They carry a grid of their own, and the coordinates are read off that grid by completing a parallelogram.

The coordinates \(1.75\) and \(0.75\) are deliberately unlovely. In the standard frame this same arrow is \((3,2)\), and neither pair is the true one. Two vectors reach everything, so the weights exist; neither is a multiple of the other, so they are unique — both halves are proved in section III.

Orthogonality

  • Two directions at a right angle
  • Skewed basis: read \(c_1\) parallel to \(b_2\) — the two coordinates come out together or not at all
  • Orthogonal basis: a perpendicular drop, one coordinate at a time

In an orthogonal basis, each coordinate can be found on its own.

Speaker notes

Same arrow in both panels. Cover the right-hand one and ask how to read \(c_1\) on the left: people reach for a perpendicular and get the wrong answer. That mistake is the slide.

Orthogonal vectors are also independent: neither can be built out of the other, since neither has any part lying along it.

Right angles are the geometry here. The next slide picks the basis that has them everywhere; later in this section they become a number — the inner product, zero exactly when two vectors are orthogonal — and the perpendicular drop becomes a formula.

Cartesian coordinates

  • An orthogonal basis, with both vectors the same length
  • Standard basis: \(e_1=(1,0)\), \(e_2=(0,1)\)
$$v=3\,e_1+1\,e_2$$

Speaker notes

Take the orthogonality of the last slide and add one more demand: that the two basis vectors agree on what counts as one step. Equivalently, \(b_2\) is \(b_1\) given a quarter turn. The grid is then square, and coordinates read against such a frame are Cartesian.

The thesis of the lecture, worth saying plainly: a computer holding \((3,1)\) is holding a description, not the arrow. Turn the frame and the same arrow reads as different numbers.

Everything from here to section III uses the standard frame by default — worth saying out loud, because it is an assumption, not a fact.

The norm

How long an arrow is.

\(\lVert u\rVert\) is the length of the arrow. Always:

  • \(\lVert c\,u\rVert=|c|\,\lVert u\rVert\)
  • \(u/\lVert u\rVert\) has length \(1\)
  • Distance is \(\lVert u-v\rVert\)

In an orthogonal basis it is Pythagoras:

$$\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\dots+u_n^2}$$

Speaker notes

Draw the right triangle with a finger: the components are the legs, the vector is the hypotenuse. That is the whole definition, and it is the reason the formula has squares in it.

Scaling: doubling a vector doubles its length, and the absolute value is there because a negative scalar reverses the arrow without shortening it.

Normalizing — dividing by the length — keeps the direction and throws away the magnitude. That is worth naming now, because it turns up constantly later: a direction of descent in lecture 3, and the scaling of attention scores in lecture 5.

Lean on the split the slide makes. The properties at the top are what a length is — they hold whatever basis you picked, and in fact they are how a norm is defined in general. The square-root formula is not the definition; it is what the length happens to work out to when the axes are perpendicular and equally scaled. Read those same components against a skewed basis and the formula gives the wrong number for the same arrow.

The inner product

One number, carrying the angle between two vectors.

$$u\cdot v=\sum_i u_iv_i=\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert\cos\theta$$
  • Also called the dot product
  • positive: acute   zero: right angle   negative: obtuse
  • \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{u\cdot u}\)

Orthogonality is now a number being zero.

Speaker notes

Work the example out loud: \(u=(3,1)\), \(v=(1,2)\), so \(u\cdot v=5\), \(\lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{10}\), \(\lVert v\rVert=\sqrt5\), and \(\cos\theta=5/\sqrt{50}=1/\sqrt2\), giving \(45^\circ\).

Note the norm falls out as the case \(v=u\): \(u\cdot u\) is the sum of squares from the last slide. So the inner product subsumes the norm rather than replacing it.

The sign is the intuition worth keeping: positive means roughly the same direction, zero at a right angle, negative roughly opposite. In lecture 5 an attention score is exactly this number — a query dotted with a key — and it means the same thing there.

Do not prove that the two formulas agree; say it is the law of cosines rearranged. One caveat worth saying out loud: the sum formula reads Cartesian coordinates — a skewed basis needs different arithmetic for the same geometry.

Orthogonal projection

The point on the line through \(v\) that is closest to \(u\).

$$p=\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v,\qquad r=u-p,\qquad r\cdot v=0$$

Every vector splits, uniquely, into a part along \(v\) and a part orthogonal to \(v\).

Speaker notes

This is the perpendicular drop of the orthogonality slide, now with a formula. That coefficient is the only one making the residual orthogonal to \(v\), which is what makes \(p\) the closest point.

Numbers: \(u=(3,1)\), \(v=(1,1)\), \(u\cdot v=4\), \(v\cdot v=2\), so \(p=2v=(2,2)\) and \(r=(1,-1)\). Check \(r\cdot v=1-1=0\).

Say the word closest out loud, because that is the form this returns in: lecture 3 solves least squares, and the answer is the projection of the data onto what the model can reach.

Close the loop with the title slide: \(p\) and \(r\) are the two pieces of \(v=(3,1)\) along the turned frame's axes there, and their lengths \(2\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt2\) are exactly the green numbers in that picture. Projection is how those numbers were found.

Systems of linear equations

Where do the lines meet?

$$\begin{aligned}2x_1+x_2&=5\\x_1+2x_2&=4\end{aligned}$$
$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}}_{x} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix}}_{b}$$

Two equations, two lines — the solution is where they cross.

Speaker notes

An equation is linear when each unknown appears once, multiplied by a number, and the results are added — no powers, no products of unknowns. In two unknowns each equation draws a line.

Two lines in the plane usually cross once. Ask what the exceptions are — parallel lines, no solution; the same line twice, infinitely many — and note that this is the entire theory of two-by-two systems, visible.

The definition of \(Ax\) is chosen to make the notation say what it means: entry \(i\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) against \(x\), which is exactly the left-hand side of equation \(i\). Read by columns it says something else worth naming — \(Ax=x_1a_1+x_2a_2\), a linear combination of the columns of \(A\). Solving means finding those weights, and that reading is the one that generalises: least squares in lecture 3 is the same sentence with \(b\) out of reach.

Say what is not in this lecture: how a system is actually solved. Elimination is the answer and it is a talk of its own; the LU slide names the factorization it produces. Today the system is here to force the notation.

The matrix product

$$(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k} A_{ik}B_{kj},\qquad (m\times n)(n\times p)=(m\times p)$$
  • \((AB)C = A(BC)\)
  • \(AB \neq BA\)

Entry \((i,j)\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) against column \(j\) of \(B\).

Speaker notes

Where it comes from: suppose \(x\) is itself produced by a system, \(x=Bz\), and we want \(y=Ax\). Writing out \(A(Bz)\) shows a single matrix does the whole job — which is also why the product is associative: both bracketings describe the same chain of substitutions.

Click one cell of \(AB\) and let the row and column light up; that is the definition, seen. The inner dimensions are what the sum runs over — both highlighted strips have the same number of entries, always.

Associativity is worth one sentence and no more: brackets may be moved, so a long product can be evaluated in any grouping. It is not obvious from the formula, and it is what lets a stack of layers in lecture 4 collapse.

Order, though, may not be changed. \(R=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\), \(S=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) give \(RS=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) but \(SR=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\). Do not try to make them mean anything yet — they come back in section III as pictures, and that is where non-commutativity stops being an algebraic accident.

The transpose

$$(A^{\mathsf T})_{ij}=A_{ji}$$
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1&4\\1&5&9\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{\mathsf T}=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&5\\4&9\end{bmatrix}$$
  • \((A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}=A\)
  • \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\)

Flip about the diagonal.

Speaker notes

Press transpose twice and note you are back where you started. Hover the diagonal: those entries never move.

The transpose is here only because the notation is needed later: orthogonal matrices in section III, symmetric ones in section IV. Do not dwell.

The reversal in \((AB)^{\mathsf T}=B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\) is worth one sentence — it is the same reversal as undoing two operations in order.

The identity matrix

$$Ix=x$$
$$I=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad AI=IA=A$$
  • Ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere

\(I\) is the \(1\) of matrix multiplication.

Speaker notes

Short slide. The defining property is the first line — it leaves every vector alone — and the array is just what that forces. As a system it says \(x_1=b_1\), \(x_2=b_2\), and so on: it asks nothing.

Check it against the column reading: \(Ie_1=e_1\), \(Ie_2=e_2\), so the columns of \(I\) are the basis vectors themselves, left where they were. In section III that becomes "the transformation that moves nothing".

Having a \(1\) is what makes it sensible to ask for reciprocals — which is the next slide.

The inverse

Undoing the effect of \(A\).

$$A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I$$
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$$
  • Multiply \(Ax=b\) by \(A^{-1}\):  \(x=A^{-1}b\)
  • Not every matrix has one
  • When it exists, it is unique

Speaker notes

A square \(A\) is invertible when some \(A^{-1}\) undoes it. Then the system has exactly one solution and we can write it down.

Uniqueness is a one-line argument if anyone wants it: if \(B\) and \(C\) both invert \(A\), then \(B=BI=B(AC)=(BA)C=IC=C\). That is associativity from the matrix-product slide doing real work.

Check the example out loud on \(b=(5,4)\): \(\tfrac13(2\cdot5-1\cdot4)=2\) and \(\tfrac13(-5+8)=1\), so \(x=(2,1)\) — the crossing point of the two lines from the systems slide.

No inverse: in \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) the second equation says nothing the first did not, and there is no way back. That matrix returns in section III as a map that flattens the plane onto a line.

Worth saying once, plainly: writing \(x=A^{-1}b\) is not how a system gets solved in practice. Forming the inverse is slower and less accurate than eliminating — the LU slide names what software does instead.

Matrices as linear transformations

A matrix moves every point of the plane.

$$T(x)=Ax$$

Every \(x\) in the plane goes to \(T(x)\).

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1.3&0.6\\-0.35&1.05\end{bmatrix}$$
  • The origin stays put: \(T(0)=0\)
  • Straight lines stay straight, parallel stays parallel
  • Evenly spaced stays evenly spaced

A square grid becomes a grid of identical parallelograms. That is all a matrix may do.

Speaker notes

The shift to make explicit: so far \(A\) has been a table of coefficients and the question was which \(x\) solves \(Ax=b\). Now feed it every \(x\) in the plane and watch where each one lands. Same arithmetic, different question, and from here on \(A\) is a thing that moves the plane.

This is the turn of the lecture, and the first time the logo appears.

Why the grid must come out like that is one line of algebra, worth saying: \(T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v)\) and \(T(cu)=c\,T(u)\) — multiplying out \(A(u+v)\) and \(A(cu)\) shows both — so a sum of steps maps to the sum of the mapped steps. Walking one grid cell across always maps to the same displacement, wherever you start. Hence identical parallelograms.

The negative statements are the useful ones: a matrix cannot bend a line and cannot move the origin. Lecture 4's first real claim — that a stack of linear layers collapses into one — is this slide and nothing more.

The columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}|&|\\Ae_1&Ae_2\\|&|\end{bmatrix},\qquad Ax=x_1\,Ae_1+x_2\,Ae_2$$

A linear map is determined by what it does to a basis.

Speaker notes

Read \(Ax\) by columns — \(Ax=x_1a_1+x_2a_2\) — and \(Ae_1\) picks out the first column of \(A\), \(Ae_2\) the second. And that is everything, because every \(x\) is a combination of \(e_1\) and \(e_2\). To find the matrix of a transformation, apply it to the basis vectors and write the answers as columns.

The load-bearing slide of the lecture. Drag one arrow onto the other and watch the figure collapse onto a line: the two columns are now dependent, the map has thrown away a whole direction, and no inverse can exist. That is \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\), live.

Then read the matrix off the two arrows out loud, once, slowly. If the room follows that, the rest of the section is bookkeeping.

Rotation

$$R_\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$$
  • Columns are \(e_1, e_2\) turned by \(\theta\)
  • Lengths and angles survive

A turn is orthogonal: \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\).

Speaker notes

Sweep the slider through a full turn: grey and red stay congruent. That is orthogonality — \((Qu)\cdot(Qv)=u^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qv=u\cdot v\), so every length and every angle is preserved.

Read the matrix off the green arrows as you sweep: the first column is where \(e_1\) lands, and that is \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) by definition of the cosine and sine. Nothing to memorise.

The readout confirms \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) at every angle. That is the algebraic statement of "nothing was stretched".

Scaling

$$S=\begin{bmatrix}s_1&0\\0&s_2\end{bmatrix}$$
  • Each axis stretched on its own
  • \(s_i=0\) throws that direction away

Speaker notes

Drive \(s_2\) to its floor and watch the figure flatten onto a line. That is the collapse from the draggable-arrows slide: the map has thrown away a whole direction, every point on a vertical line now lands on the same spot, and no inverse can exist.

A negative scale is a reflection in the other axis — worth a two-second mention, no slide of its own.

Shear

$$H_k=\begin{bmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$$
  • \(e_1\) fixed, \(e_2\) slides sideways
  • Each row slides further than the one below

Straight lines stay straight — but angles do not survive.

Speaker notes

Sweep and narrate: the figure leans, the horizontal axis is untouched, and points further up slide further across. Lengths and angles have obviously changed, so a shear is not orthogonal — unlike the rotation two slides back.

Worth pointing out on the figure: the grid cells are still identical parallelograms, just leaning ones. A shear is as linear as a rotation.

Translation is the odd one out

  • It moves the origin — no linear map may do that
  • Append a coordinate equal to \(1\), and it is a matrix again
$$\begin{bmatrix}1&0&t_1\\0&1&t_2\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}x_1+t_1\\x_2+t_2\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

Linear plus a shift is called affine.

Speaker notes

Move the sliders and point at the ringed origin: it has left home, which is exactly what the linearity slide forbade. So translation is not a two-by-two matrix, and no amount of choosing entries will make it one.

The trick — homogeneous coordinates — is to work one dimension up. The three-by-three readout is what graphics hardware does, and it is how lecture 4 folds a bias term into an affine layer.

Combining transforms

$$R=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad T=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&t_1\\0&1&t_2\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$$
  • Rightmost acts first
  • \(TR\): turn, then move
  • \(RT\): move, then turn — the shift gets turned too

Matrix multiplication is composition.

Speaker notes

Say the reading rule out loud first, because it is the thing people get backwards: in \(AB\) the rightmost matrix acts first. \(TRx\) means do \(R\) to \(x\), then \(T\) to the result — the order you read the symbols is the reverse of the order things happen.

Set \(\theta\) to \(60^\circ\) and \(t_1\) to \(2\), then flip between the buttons and watch the ringed origin. Turn-then-move puts the figure out along the \(x\)-axis; move-then-turn swings that whole displacement around, so the figure ends up somewhere else entirely. The translation itself got rotated.

This is \(AB\neq BA\) from the matrix-product slide, in pictures. Point at the readout as you flip: the third column — where the origin goes — is what changes.

Note both factors are \(3\times3\). That is the homogeneous trick from the last slide earning its keep: once translation is a matrix, turns and shifts compose by ordinary matrix multiplication, which is exactly why graphics pipelines use it.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The directions a map does not turn.

$$Av=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq0$$
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$$ $$\begin{aligned} A\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} &=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\\[6pt] A\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix} &=1\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

An eigenvector is a direction the map only stretches.

Speaker notes

Almost every vector comes out of \(A\) pointing somewhere else. A nonzero \(v\) that does not is an eigenvector, and the factor is the eigenvalue.

Do the multiplication out loud so the pattern is earned: \(A(1,1)\) is \((2+1,\,1+2)=(3,3)\), which is \(3\) times what went in. Then \(A(-1,1)\) is \((-2+1,\,-1+2)=(-1,1)\), the same vector back, so \(\lambda=1\). Do not compute a characteristic polynomial; we have not done determinants, and it is not needed to see the idea.

The two eigendirections here are perpendicular. That is not luck: \(A\) is symmetric, and symmetric matrices always have an orthogonal set of eigenvectors.

Two caveats, named rather than developed: \(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\) has only one eigendirection instead of two, and a rotation has no real eigenvector at all.

Where they go: in lecture 3 the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian is the condition number, and it predicts how badly steepest descent zig-zags.

LU

Splitting a matrix into triangular factors.

$$A=LU$$
  • A factorization: one matrix written as a product of matrices with a useful shape
  • Triangular systems solve by substitution, one unknown at a time
  • How numerical software actually solves \(Ax=b\)

Speaker notes

Lead with the idea, because it is the first factorization in the series: a matrix can be written as a product of matrices whose shape makes a problem easy. Not smaller, not simpler in any general sense — triangular, which is precisely the shape substitution can exploit. The same move, splitting an object into factors chosen to suit the task, is behind every decomposition people meet later.

Why triangular helps: the last row of \(Ux=y\) involves one unknown, so read it off; substitute upward and each row in turn has one unknown left. No search, no iteration, one pass. Same going down for \(L\).

So \(Ax=b\) becomes \(LUx=b\): solve \(Ly=b\) downward, then \(Ux=y\) upward. And since \(L\) and \(U\) do not depend on \(b\), one factorization serves any number of right-hand sides. That is the payoff against the inverse slide — this, not \(A^{-1}\), is what a library call does.

Point at the shapes: between them \(L\) and \(U\) hold as many numbers as \(A\) did. Nothing was added; the elimination steps were just written down. Elimination itself is a lecture of its own and is deliberately not given here.

Quiz

Four questions, hands up.

Speaker notes

Multiple choice, answered from the room — nobody needs paper. Read each one out, count hands for each letter, then say the answer. If time is short, ask 1 and 3: they cover what lectures 3, 4 and 5 lean on hardest.

What is \(u\cdot v\)?

$$u=(2,-1),\qquad v=(1,2)$$
  1. A\(0\) — they are orthogonal
  2. B\(4\) — the angle is acute
  3. C\(-4\) — the angle is obtuse
  4. DCannot tell without the lengths

Speaker notes

Answer: A. \(2\cdot1+(-1)\cdot2=0\). Zero means a right angle, so \(\theta=90^\circ\). Note nobody needed the lengths — that is the point of the inner product.

Which picture is not a linear map?

  1. AA rotation about the origin
  2. BA shear
  3. CA translation
  4. DA scaling that flattens the plane onto a line

Speaker notes

Answer: C. A translation moves the origin, which no linear map may do. D is perfectly linear — it just has no inverse. Recognising that "not invertible" and "not linear" are different complaints is the whole question.

A map sends \(e_1\mapsto(0,1)\) and \(e_2\mapsto(-2,0)\). What is its matrix?

  1. A\(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-2&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\)
  2. B\(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\)
  3. C\(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&-2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\)
  4. D\(\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}-2&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\)

Speaker notes

Answer: B. The images go in as columns, not rows — A is the same numbers laid in as rows, and is the trap. This map turns a quarter turn counterclockwise and stretches the second direction by two. If the room splits between A and B, the draggable-arrows slide is the one to go back to.

What is an eigenvector of a matrix \(A\)?

  1. AA vector that \(A\) leaves completely unchanged
  2. BA vector whose direction \(A\) does not change
  3. CA vector that \(A\) sends to the origin
  4. DThe longest vector \(A\) produces

Speaker notes

Answer: B. It stays on its own line through the origin; \(A\) may stretch or shrink it, and the factor is the eigenvalue. A is too strong — that is only the case \(\lambda=1\). C is the case \(\lambda=0\), a special eigenvector rather than the definition. D is not a thing.

Follow-up if they are quick: does a rotation have one? No real one — every direction turns. That is worth ending on, because it shows the definition has teeth.
